Two Different Things Share This Name

Type "safety stock photo" into a search bar and you get two answers mixed together. One is a picture — a hard hat, a warning sign, a shield icon — the kind of image a blog post or a training deck needs. The other is a number — the buffer of inventory a business holds so it doesn't run dry when a shipment is late or demand jumps. Same words, unrelated meanings.

If you landed here chasing a photo, iStock and the other stock libraries have millions of images tagged under safety, hazard, and protective equipment, and that's genuinely a fine place to look — a photo library is what it's built to be. If you landed here because you're staring at a reorder point on a spreadsheet, keep reading. This page is about that number.

What Safety Stock Actually Is

Safety stock is the extra inventory you carry on top of what you expect to sell before your next shipment lands. It exists because two things are never perfectly predictable: how fast units actually sell, and how long a supplier or freight forwarder actually takes to deliver. Without a buffer, any delay or demand spike turns into a stockout — and on Amazon a stockout does more damage than a lost sale. It can drop organic rank mid-campaign, and clawing that rank back once the listing is buyable again usually takes longer than the stockout itself.

Safety stock is not the same thing as your reorder point. The reorder point is safety stock plus the demand you expect to sell during the lead time. Safety stock is the cushion layered on top of that expected demand, not a replacement for it.

The Formula, Worked With Real Numbers

The version most FBA sellers actually use compares a worst-case scenario to an average one:

Safety Stock = (Max Daily Sales × Max Lead Time) − (Average Daily Sales × Average Lead Time)

Here's what that looks like with numbers instead of letters. Say a SKU sells 40 units a day on average, but on its busiest days it moves 65. Lead time from factory to an FBA warehouse averages 20 days, but the worst delay in the last year stretched to 35.

(65 × 35) − (40 × 20) = 2,275 − 800 = 1,475 units of safety stock.

That's the buffer held in addition to the stock expected to sell during a normal lead time, not instead of it. Sellers who want a more statistically rigorous number use a service-level formula built on the standard deviation of demand and lead time — it accounts for variability more precisely, but it needs several months of clean sales and lead-time data to be worth the extra math. The max-min version above is good enough for most single-SKU decisions and can be sanity-checked without a statistics background.

The Mistake Almost Everyone Makes

The most common error is plugging in average lead time everywhere instead of the worst-case figure. It produces a smaller, tidier number — and it's wrong in exactly the direction that hurts: it understates the buffer right before the delay that actually happens. We've watched this trip up inventory operations at every scale, including inside the $500M+ in Amazon spend Full Circle has managed across 100+ brands.

We've made a version of this mistake ourselves: recalculating a reorder point off the prior quarter's average lead time days after a carrier disruption, before the new delay pattern had fully shown up in the shipping data. The number looked clean. It was already stale by the time it went live.

The second common error is calculating safety stock once and never touching it again. Lead times shift with carriers, ports, and supplier capacity. Demand variability shifts with seasonality and with your own advertising spend. A number that was right in March can be wrong by August.

When the Number Is Wrong, or the Fix Didn't Work

If you've set a safety stock level and you're still getting stockouts, check three things before assuming the formula failed.

  • Confirm it's actually applied. Some systems default the reorder point to zero safety stock unless you set it explicitly at the SKU level. It's easy to assume it's on when it isn't.
  • Check whether your "maximum lead time" is still current. If a longer delay has happened since you last updated the input, the whole calculation is running on an outdated worst case.
  • Check whether the spike is organic or ad-driven. If a PPC campaign pushed sales faster than any forecast accounted for, that's a different problem, and it usually sits on the ad side rather than the inventory side — that's Dr. PPC territory, not an inventory formula.

If the number comes out negative, your average case already covers your worst case, and you likely don't need much of a buffer for that SKU right now — though it's worth revisiting after a seasonal spike or a supplier change.

Common questions

Is safety stock the same as buffer stock?

Mostly, yes — the terms are used interchangeably in practice. Where people draw a distinction, "buffer stock" sometimes refers to a fixed cushion set by judgment, while "safety stock" refers to one calculated from lead time and demand variability. On most Amazon seller spreadsheets, they mean the same thing.

Where can I actually find stock photos of safety equipment?

iStock and similar image libraries carry millions of photos and illustrations tagged under safety, hazard signage, and protective equipment. That's genuinely what those sites are built for — it's a different tool for a different job than the inventory calculation on this page.

What's a more rigorous formula than max-min?

A service-level formula: safety stock equals a Z-score (tied to your target service level, like 95%) multiplied by the standard deviation of demand during lead time. It's more precise about variability but needs enough clean sales and lead-time history to calculate the standard deviation meaningfully — usually several months of data at minimum.

How often should safety stock be recalculated?

Whenever lead time or demand variability shifts meaningfully — after a carrier disruption, a supplier change, a new peak season, or a big change in ad spend on that SKU. Treating it as a one-time setting is the second most common mistake sellers make with this number.

What if I don't have enough sales history to calculate it properly?

Use a judgment-based buffer sized off category norms or a comparable SKU until you have several weeks of clean sales and lead-time data, then switch to the calculated version. A rough number applied consistently beats no number at all.
