What an Amazon reimbursement claim actually is

When you pay Amazon to fulfill your inventory, you're buying a set of promises: units get received accurately, stored safely, picked correctly, and returned or disposed of the way you asked. A reimbursement claim is what you file when one of those promises breaks — a unit vanishes between the truck and the shelf, gets crushed by a forklift, gets charged the wrong fee tier, or gets refunded to a customer without coming back into your stock.

Amazon reimburses some of this automatically. It does not find all of it. The system is built to process what's easy to detect — a unit marked damaged with a photo, a return that's clearly missing — and leaves the rest for you to catch by comparing reports. That gap between what Amazon reimburses on its own and what it actually owes you is the entire reason this process exists.

The five discrepancy types, and what proof each one needs

Amazon groups reimbursement-eligible errors into a handful of categories. Each one needs a different report as evidence, and each has its own effective deadline for filing.

How Amazon calculates what it owes you — a worked example

Say you ship 500 units to a fulfillment center. The receiving report shows 485 confirmed. Fifteen units sit in limbo. If they're still unaccounted for after roughly 30 days, they become eligible for a lost-inventory claim, and Amazon's inventory reconciliation report is where you'll find them, listed by ASIN and quantity.

Amazon doesn't reimburse at your list price. It uses the median price the item sold for, yours and comparable sellers', over a trailing window, and takes the current list price into account if sales history is thin. If that median works out to $28 a unit, 15 units is a $420 claim — small on its own, but multiply that across a catalog and a year and it adds up fast. There's also a ceiling: Amazon caps reimbursement at $5,000 per unit in the US (£2,000 in the UK), which matters if you sell anything high-value.

Across the $500M+ in Amazon spend Full Circle has managed for 100+ brands, the pattern is consistent: the real money isn't in the dramatic damaged-carton photo. It's in the slow drip of units that go quietly missing during a warehouse transfer and never resurface on anyone's dashboard until someone reconciles the report line by line.

How to file — the actual steps

  • Pull the right report first. Inventory reconciliation report for lost/damaged units, FBA returns report cross-checked against refund reports for mishandled returns, and the FBA fee preview compared to actual charges for fee disputes.
  • Isolate discrepancies older than the grace window. Amazon needs time to self-correct — filing too early gets you an automatic rejection, not a real answer.
  • Open the case with specifics. ASIN, quantity, shipment or FC ID, and the report line the discrepancy came from. Vague cases get vague denials.
  • For fee disputes, attach your own product measurements against what Amazon billed — dimensional-weight misclassification is common and it repeats on every single unit sold until it's fixed, not just once.
  • Track the case number and follow up. Amazon's queue moves slower than sellers expect, and cases that go stale get closed with no resolution.

When the number is wrong: denials, underpayments, and the mistakes that cost sellers money

Amazon will sometimes deny a claim by saying the unit is accounted for, even when your own ledger disagrees. Don't take that as final. Re-pull the inventory ledger for the exact date range, attach it, and reopen. Denials based on a stale report snapshot get reversed more often than sellers assume.

The single most expensive mistake is missing the window. Once a discrepancy passes roughly 18 months old, it's generally not eligible anymore — full stop, no dispute process reverses that. Sellers who reconcile once a quarter instead of continuously lose claims this way, quietly, with no denial notice to alert them.

The second mistake — one we've made too — is over-indexing on lost and damaged units because they're visible and emotionally satisfying to chase, while skipping the dimensional-weight and fee-tier audit that's usually worth more per SKU over a year. A damaged carton is a one-time claim. A fee misclassification is a leak on every unit you sell until someone catches it.

Common questions

How long do I have to file an Amazon reimbursement claim?

Generally within 18 months of the discrepancy. After that window closes, the claim is effectively dead regardless of how solid your evidence is. This is the single most common reason sellers lose money they were genuinely owed — not denial, just expiry.

Does Amazon reimburse automatically, or do I always need to file?

Some cases resolve automatically, particularly clear-cut warehouse damage with a photo on file. Most lost-inventory, fee, and return discrepancies require you to notice the gap yourself and open a manual case with Amazon's own reports as proof.

What's the maximum Amazon will reimburse for one unit?

Amazon caps reimbursement at $5,000 per unit in the US and £2,000 in the UK. If you sell anything priced above that cap, the reimbursement won't make you whole, which matters for insurance and pricing decisions on high-value SKUs.

What do I do if Amazon denies my claim?

Re-pull the relevant report for the exact date range in question and reopen the case with that data attached. Denials are frequently based on a report snapshot that doesn't match your own ledger, and a lot of them get reversed on a second, better-documented attempt.

Should I file reimbursement claims myself or use a service?

If your catalog is small and you have time to reconcile reports weekly, doing it yourself is entirely workable. Specialist recovery services and broader inventory-management products both exist for sellers who'd rather not run that reconciliation loop by hand — the right choice depends on whether reimbursement is your only leak or one of several.
