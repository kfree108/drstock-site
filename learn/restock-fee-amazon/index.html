What Amazon's Restocking Fee Actually Covers

The restocking fee on Amazon is not a general charge for returning something you didn't want. Amazon's own return policy limits it to a short list: opened or activated software and video games, and opened collectible cards, board games, or collectible figurines. If your return falls in one of those categories, the fee is 100% of the item price — you get nothing back, not a partial deduction.

Worked example: you buy a $60 video game, open the case, activate the code, then decide you don't want it. You return it. The restocking fee for opened software and games is 100%, so the refund is $0, not $48 or $30. There's no sliding scale here — the fee either applies at full price or it doesn't apply at all.

People often lump three separate charges under the same phrase: late fee (missed the return-by date — 20% of price for the first 30 days late, 100% after that), damage fee (returned used, missing parts, or tags removed — up to 50% of price, 100% for Amazon Luxury), and the restocking fee itself, which only exists for that narrow software/games/collectibles list. If a refund came back short, the fee name on your return confirmation tells you which of these three you're actually looking at.

The Mistake Almost Everyone Makes With This Fee

The common mistake on the buyer side is assuming the restocking fee is broad. A shopper sees a deduction and calls it a restocking fee when it's actually the damage fee or the late fee — different trigger, different math, different argument to make. Arguing 'I didn't restock anything' won't help if the actual charge was for dropping the return off four days late.

On the seller side, the mistake runs the other way. Brands sometimes assume this consumer-facing restocking fee is the same thing Amazon deducts from a seller's settlement when a customer returns an FBA item. It isn't. FBA sellers carry their own separate cost — a returns processing fee that applies to specific categories, deducted from the seller's account rather than the buyer's refund, and banded by category rather than charged as a flat percentage. If you're reconciling a settlement report and see a return-related deduction, check which fee it actually is before assuming Amazon got it wrong — and check again if it doesn't match the category rules, because these do get misapplied.

When Does Amazon Restock — A Different Question Entirely

'When will Amazon restock this' isn't a fee question — it's an inventory question, and Amazon doesn't publish a restock schedule because Amazon usually isn't the one deciding it. Most listings belong to a specific seller, sometimes Amazon retail, more often a third-party brand, and that seller controls when more units go in. 'Currently unavailable' can mean back in two days or not at all, depending on that seller's supply chain — not on any Amazon-wide calendar.

If you're the seller asking when you restock, the honest answer is: it depends on your lead time, your reorder point, and how much runway you gave yourself before you ran out. This is where a stockout starts costing more than the sale you missed — an ASIN that goes out of stock loses organic rank, and getting that rank back after restocking takes real time and ad spend, not a click.

What To Do If the Fee — or the Restock Timing — Looks Wrong

If you're a shopper and a fee was applied that you think is wrong — the item wasn't actually opened, or it was dropped off before the return-by date — go to Your Orders, find the return, and contact Customer Service naming the specific fee you're disputing. Don't dispute 'the restocking fee' broadly if the line item is actually the damage fee; the rep is working from the same categorized fee table you now have.

If you're a seller and a return-related deduction on your settlement report doesn't match the category rules, treat it like any FBA fee dispute: pull the ASIN, pull the category, pull the settlement line, and file with those specifics rather than a general complaint. Generic disputes get generic, slow answers.

And if the real problem is that units aren't getting back into stock fast enough after a return or removal decision, that's not a fee dispute at all — it's a reorder-timing problem sitting upstream of everything above.

Common questions

Does Amazon charge a restocking fee on all returns?

No. Most returns are free within the 30-day window. The specific 'restocking fee' line only applies to opened software, video games, and certain opened collectibles, and it's charged at 100% of the item price rather than a partial deduction.

When will Amazon restock an item marked 'currently unavailable'?

There's no published Amazon-wide restock date. Most listings belong to a specific seller, and restocking depends on that seller's own supply chain and reorder timing, not a schedule Amazon controls or discloses to shoppers.

Is the seller-side FBA returns processing fee the same as the buyer-facing restocking fee?

No. The restocking fee described on Amazon's return policy page is charged to the shopper and reduces their refund, and it only applies to a narrow list of categories. The FBA returns processing fee is a separate, seller-side deduction on certain categories, taken from the seller's settlement rather than the buyer's refund.

What if I think I was charged a restocking fee unfairly?

Check your return confirmation for the exact fee name — restocking, late, or damage fee — since each has a different trigger and different math. Then contact Customer Service naming that specific fee rather than filing a general overcharge complaint.

Why did my item go out of stock and stay that way for weeks?

That's usually a reorder-timing problem, not a policy question. It often means the reorder point was set too late relative to lead time, and once an ASIN goes out of stock it can lose organic rank that takes longer to rebuild than the stockout itself lasted.
