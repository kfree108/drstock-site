What Amazon Lead Time Actually Means

"Lead time" on Amazon covers at least three separate clocks, and mixing them up is the single most common mistake we see.

  • Seller Central lead time to ship — a setting under handling time that tells Amazon how many days you need to ship an order after it's placed. It feeds the estimated delivery date buyers see and affects Buy Box eligibility.
  • Vendor Central PO lead time — the number of days a vendor has contractually committed to Amazon's buyer to ship product after a purchase order is issued. This is a negotiated term, not a physical measurement.
  • Replenishment lead time — the real-world clock: from placing a reorder with your supplier to having sellable stock actually sitting in an Amazon warehouse.

People searching "eye lead time" are almost always typing or hearing "lead time" wrong — autocomplete and voice search mangle it the same way, and the search volume shows up as its own phrase. The underlying question doesn't change: how long between reordering and having stock Amazon can actually sell.

How to Calculate Real Replenishment Lead Time

Say a supplier confirms 20 days production after you place a PO. Ocean freight runs 15 days port to port, plus 4 days of drayage and trucking to the Amazon gateway. Add Amazon's receiving queue — call it 7 days in a normal month — and total replenishment lead time is 46 days, not the 20 days on the supplier's quote.

If you sell 10 units a day, 46 days of lead time means you need 460 units in the pipeline just to cover the gap, before adding any safety stock for demand spikes or supplier slippage. Most reorder-point math we see stops at the supplier's number and ignores the other two legs completely.

Across the $500M+ in managed revenue we've managed across 100+ brands, the receiving leg — days between a shipment hitting an Amazon dock and units actually being checked in and available to sell — is the one brands underestimate hardest. A routine 5- to 7-day receiving window can stretch to two or three weeks heading into Q4, and there's no field in Seller Central that warns you in advance.

Where the Number Goes Wrong

The most common failure isn't a bad calculation — it's a missing leg. A seasonal brand like Epic Gardening feels this hardest: a lead time that's accurate in February is wrong by the time spring demand hits, because the receiving queue behind it has changed, not just the sales velocity.

  • Treating the supplier's quote as the whole answer. Production time is one leg of three.
  • Setting Seller Central lead time to ship too aggressively to win Buy Box, then missing it — that hits Late Shipment Rate and Order Defect Rate.
  • Using a single point estimate with no safety stock for variance in any leg.
  • Confusing Vendor Central PO lead time (a contract term) with actual fulfillment reality.

We've made this mistake too: building a reorder point off a supplier's 20-day quote without validating actual receiving times against past shipments, and watching a SKU go out of stock two weeks before the restock landed. The fix isn't a smarter formula. It's checking the receiving leg against your own shipment history instead of assuming it holds steady.

What to Do When the Lead Time Number Is Wrong

If your calculated lead time turns out shorter than reality, don't rebuild the formula — pull actual received-versus-shipped dates from your Seller Central shipment reports and recalculate from what actually happened, not what was quoted.

If Late Shipment Rate is climbing because the lead time to ship setting is too tight, widen it. A small Buy Box hit from a more honest handling-time setting is smaller than an account health strike.

If restock limits or IPI are choking replenishment because lead time was underestimated, the fix is safety stock built into the number, not a tighter point estimate. And if a stockout keeps happening two to three weeks after you thought stock had landed, check carton-level check-in times per shipment — not just the carrier's "delivered" scan, which only tells you it reached the dock, not that it's sellable.

Common questions

What's a good lead time on Amazon?

There's no single benchmark — it depends on category, supplier location, and shipping method. What matters more than hitting a target number is knowing your actual total lead time, all three legs, rather than working off the supplier's production quote alone.

Does lead time affect the Buy Box or delivery estimates?

Yes, for seller-fulfilled orders. The lead time to ship setting in Seller Central feeds directly into the estimated delivery date buyers see, which is a factor in Buy Box eligibility. Setting it too optimistically to win the box, then missing it, damages Late Shipment Rate instead.

Is "eye lead time" a real Amazon term?

No. It's a typo or mishearing of "lead time," most likely from voice search or autocomplete. The underlying question — how long between reordering and having sellable stock — is identical to the standard search for Amazon lead time.

How does IPI or restock limits relate to lead time?

Amazon's Inventory Performance Index and any restock limits it imposes are calculated partly on how efficiently you're managing stock levels. Underestimating lead time leads to either stockouts (hurting sell-through history) or over-ordering to compensate (hurting storage efficiency) — both feed back into the score.

What's the difference between vendor lead time and seller lead time?

Vendor Central PO lead time is a contracted commitment a 1P vendor makes to Amazon's buying team about shipping after a PO is issued — it's a negotiated term. Seller Central lead time to ship is an operational setting a 3P seller controls that determines delivery estimates. They describe different relationships with Amazon and aren't interchangeable.
