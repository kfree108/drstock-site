Two Different Things Called 'Restock Alert'

Most people typing this phrase mean one of two unrelated systems. If you're a shopper, it's the email notification you get when an out-of-stock product comes back for sale. If you're a seller, it's the internal signal that's supposed to warn you before you run out - the one that protects your rank and your buy box.

Amazon's own help page only covers the shopper version: the 'Alert Me' box on a product page. Third-party sites like backinstockalerts.com do the same job from outside Amazon, watching a URL and emailing you when it changes. Neither one touches the seller side, which lives entirely inside Seller Central and has nothing to do with shopper notifications.

This page covers both, but spends more time on the seller side, because that's where the real cost sits. A shopper missing a restocked kettle is an inconvenience. A seller missing their own restock window can mean a week of lost organic rank that takes a month to earn back.

How to Get a Restock Alert as a Shopper

On the product page, look for an Alert Me box, usually below the buy box. It only shows up on listings Amazon has enabled it for - not every out-of-stock item gets one. Enter your email and select sign up. That's the entire process.

  • Signing up doesn't reserve the item. Amazon says this directly: stock can sell out again in the time between the email landing and you clicking through.
  • The price quoted in the email is a snapshot, not a promise. It may have changed by the time you check out.
  • If there's no Alert Me box, a third-party tool such as backinstockalerts.com can watch the listing URL and email you when the page changes. It isn't affiliated with Amazon and works by scraping the page, so the same price-lag and 'someone else got there first' risk applies - sometimes with more delay than Amazon's own version.

If you've signed up and heard nothing, check whether the item was discontinued rather than restocked. Amazon's own help notes that some items simply stop coming back.

How to Set a Restock Alert as a Seller (With the Math That Actually Matters)

Seller Central has two places this lives: the Restock Inventory dashboard under Manage Inventory Health, and low-stock notifications tied to your Inventory Performance Index. Both flag units based on how fast you're selling versus how much you have on hand. Neither one knows your actual supplier lead time unless you tell it.

That gap is the whole problem. Here's the calculation a restock alert should really be running, worked on real numbers:

  • You sell 500 units a month - roughly 16.7 units a day.
  • Your supplier's real lead time, door to Amazon's dock, is 45 days.
  • You want 15 days of safety stock on top of that for delays and demand spikes.
  • Reorder point = (lead time + safety stock) x daily velocity = (45 + 15) x 16.7 ≈ 1,000 units.

That means the alert should fire when you're down to roughly 1,000 units, not when you're down to a few days of stock. Amazon's default low-inventory flags typically run on a fixed days-of-supply window, which can be far shorter than your real lead time - fine for a supplier three days away, dangerous for one 45 days out. We've watched this pattern across the $500M+ in Amazon spend we manage: the SKUs that go dark mid-campaign are almost never the ones with no alert at all. They're the ones where the alert fired on Amazon's default clock instead of the supplier's real one.

The Mistake That Breaks Restock Alerts (We've Made It Too)

The most common error is setting the reorder point on average lead time instead of worst-case lead time. Average lead time looks fine on a spreadsheet until one shipment gets held at customs or a factory runs a delay - and the alert that was supposed to give you 15 days of runway gives you three.

We've done this ourselves: using a supplier's quoted lead time instead of their actual historical performance, and getting caught when a single delayed shipment ate the entire safety buffer. The fix is unglamorous but it works - track actual lead time per shipment, not the number in the vendor contract, and set the reorder point on the slowest realistic case, not the average one.

On the shopper side, the equivalent mistake is treating an Alert Me signup as a guarantee. It isn't a wishlist and it isn't a hold. If the item matters, check back manually once the email lands rather than assuming it's still there.

When the Alert Fires Too Late or Not At All

If a seller-side restock alert fired and you're already stocked out, the threshold was wrong for that SKU. Recalculate the reorder point using this SKU's current velocity and its real lead time, not last quarter's numbers. Seasonal SKUs need this redone before their season starts, not during it.

If a shopper-side Alert Me signup never emails you, two things are usually true: the item was discontinued rather than restocked, or it came back for a few hours and sold out again before the email went out. Neither is a bug - it's an email notification competing with checkout speed on a popular item.

If IPI-linked notifications aren't showing up in Seller Central at all, check notification preferences under account settings. They're opt-in and easy to have switched off without noticing.

Which one you should actually pick

Amazon's built-in Alert Me box is fine for a casual shopper; third-party tools cover the gaps when it's missing. Seller Central's default low-stock notice is workable for short-lead-time sellers, but anyone with a real supplier lead time needs a custom reorder point - and a way to keep recalculating it as demand shifts, which is where a dedicated inventory system, including Dr. Stock, earns its place.

Common questions

Does every Amazon product have a restock alert option for shoppers?

No. The Alert Me box only appears on listings Amazon has enabled it for, and Amazon's own help page is explicit that not every out-of-stock item qualifies. If it's missing, a third-party URL-tracking tool is the usual workaround, with the same price-lag caveats.

Can sellers set a custom restock alert threshold in Seller Central?

Yes, through the replenishment settings in the Restock Inventory tool, where you can override Amazon's default assumptions with your actual supplier lead time. Most sellers never touch this setting, which is why the default fires later than it should for anyone with a long lead time.

What's the difference between an IPI-linked notice and a reorder point alert?

The IPI-linked notice uses a fixed days-of-supply window that Amazon sets the same way across sellers. A reorder point alert is calculated from your specific lead time plus safety stock, so it fires at the right unit count for your supply chain rather than a generic one.

Is backinstockalerts.com safe to use for a shopper alert?

It's a legitimate third-party tool - you give it a product URL and your email, and it emails you when the page changes. It has no affiliation with Amazon, so treat it the same way as Amazon's own Alert Me feature: useful for a heads-up, not a guarantee of price or availability.

How far ahead of running out should a seller's restock alert fire?

At minimum, at your lead time plus your safety stock buffer, converted into units using your daily sales velocity. A 45-day lead time with 15 days of safety stock and 500 units sold a month works out to roughly 1,000 units remaining, not a few days of stock left.
