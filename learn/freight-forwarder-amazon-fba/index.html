What a freight forwarder for Amazon FBA actually does

A freight forwarder books the transport, consolidates or containers your cargo, and files the export and import paperwork so your goods legally cross a border and land at a port, a 3PL, or an Amazon fulfillment center. Most forwarders also act as your customs broker — same skill set, one invoice instead of two.

Below roughly 200kg, you generally don't need a separate forwarder at all. A courier like DHL, FedEx, or UPS does the forwarder's job and the broker's job in one small-parcel service, and bills you for duties afterward. This is why most sellers ship their first test order by courier and only meet a forwarder once volume grows.

A forwarder is not a 3PL. The forwarder's job ends at a port or a warehouse door; a 3PL then unpacks, inspects, labels, and preps cartons for Amazon. Some forwarders bolt prep services on top, but the two functions are legally separate — a customs bond has nothing to do with whether your FNSKU labels are in the right spot.

It's also not Amazon Global Logistics. AGL is Amazon acting as the forwarder for freight from China and Vietnam into its own fulfillment and AWD network, booked inside Seller Central. It's a real freight forwarder relationship, just one where Amazon is the vendor — and it only covers freight bound for Amazon's own network. If you also stock a DTC warehouse or other marketplaces, that inventory still needs an independent forwarder.

The real decision isn't weight — it's transit time against your reorder point

The 200kg rule of thumb gets you to the right transport mode. It doesn't get you to the right reorder timing, and that's where stockouts actually happen.

Say you're reordering 1,000 units, 600kg, by LCL sea freight. Standard ocean transit typically runs 25–35 days door-to-port, plus 5–10 days for customs clearance and drayage to the fulfillment center. That's 30–45 days minimum before the units are sellable. If your reorder point was built around a 21-day assumption because that's how long the last shipment took, you're already short by 10 to 25 days before the forwarder makes a single mistake.

Air freight buys that time back — carriers quote roughly 7–10 days into the fulfillment network — at a real cost premium per kilogram. That's a legitimate trade to make on purpose when a stockout is close. It's a bad trade to discover after the fact, which is how most sellers actually encounter it.

What forwarders quote you, and why the number never sits still

There is no standard rate. A quote is built from weight, volume, lane, season, fuel surcharge, and the Incoterm you agreed with your supplier (FOB vs. EXW changes who pays for origin pickup). You'll see round per-kilogram figures quoted in seller forums and guides — treat those as illustrative, not a number you can hold a forwarder to, because lane pricing moves with fuel and container demand.

Ask for the quote broken into freight, origin handling, destination customs clearance, duty (driven by the HS code on your commercial invoice), and final delivery to the fulfillment center. An all-in single figure that doesn't separate those five things is the easiest way to get surprised by a bill after the goods have already landed.

  • Do they carry a customs bond? Required for formal entry; ask if it's theirs or a third party's.
  • Is cargo insurance included or separate? Sea freight damage claims are far more common than sellers expect.
  • Have they actually delivered to an Amazon FC before? Appointment scheduling and carton marking rules trip up forwarders who mostly serve retail warehouses.

The common mistakes — including the one we watch happen most often

The first mistake is booking on the cheapest quote without checking what it actually covers — freight to the port is not freight to the fulfillment center door, and the gap between those two is often where the real cost hides.

The second is choosing EXW because it sounds simpler, then finding out the quote didn't include origin pickup, or that the factory's idea of "ready for pickup" doesn't match the forwarder's.

The third is the one we see most, across the $500M+ in Amazon spend we track across 100+ brands: it's rarely the shipment that was late. It's the reorder point that was never updated after a lane's average transit time quietly crept up — set once, left alone, and wrong by the time it mattered.

When the answer is bad news — a shipment stuck in customs, cargo that arrives short or damaged — don't wait it out. Get the specific hold reason in writing (HS code mismatch, missing inspection certificate, an agency flag) so you fix the cause, not just this one shipment. If you bought cargo insurance, file the claim inside the forwarder's stated window — most run a claims deadline measured in days after delivery, not weeks.

Where this sits next to your inventory numbers, not instead of them

A forwarder solves the transport problem. It doesn't tell you whether the reorder point behind that shipment was right, whether the FBA fee on the SKU it's carrying was calculated correctly, or whether last quarter's shipment discrepancy ever got reimbursed. Which one you should actually pick

Courier for anything under ~200kg or a first test order. Independent freight forwarder for regular LCL or FCL volume, especially if you stock more than Amazon. Amazon Global Logistics for sellers shipping almost exclusively China or Vietnam to FBA/AWD who want one bill in Seller Central. A 3PL is a different job entirely — prep and fulfillment, not transport — and no forwarder replaces it.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Total three numbers first: storage and aged-inventory surcharges for the last twelve months, lost sales on days your best sellers were out of stock, and cash sitting in SKUs that have not moved in 180 days. Then ask each vendor what they would do about those three in week one.

Common questions

How much does a freight forwarder cost for Amazon FBA?

There's no fixed rate — it's quoted per shipment based on weight, volume, lane, season, and the Incoterm you agreed with your supplier. Round per-kilogram figures you see in seller guides are illustrative and move with fuel surcharges and container demand, so ask for a quote broken into freight, origin handling, destination customs clearance, duty, and final delivery rather than accepting one all-in number.

What's the difference between a freight forwarder and a customs broker?

A broker's one job is clearing goods through customs — filing the right documentation so cargo isn't held at the border. A forwarder's job is the whole transport chain: booking, routing, and delivery. Most forwarders include brokerage so you deal with one company, but they're functionally distinct roles.

Is Amazon Global Logistics a freight forwarder?

Yes, functionally — Amazon books and manages ocean or air freight from China and Vietnam into its own fulfillment and AWD network, the same job an independent forwarder does. The trade-off is scope: it only covers freight headed into Amazon's network, so sellers who also stock a DTC warehouse or other marketplaces still need an independent forwarder for that inventory.

Do I need a freight forwarder for a small shipment?

Under roughly 200kg, most sellers are better off letting the supplier book a courier — DHL, FedEx, or UPS — which acts as forwarder and broker in one service and bills duties directly. A dedicated forwarder starts earning its cost above that threshold, particularly on LCL or full-container sea freight.

What do I do if my shipment is stuck in customs?

Ask the forwarder for the specific hold reason in writing — a mismatched HS code, a missing inspection certificate, or an agency flag — rather than just waiting for release. That documentation tells you whether it's a one-time error or something that will recur on the next shipment from the same lane.
