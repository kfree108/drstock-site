When Does Amazon Restock Items?

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1372 words · Written against what currently ranked for “when does amazon restock items”
The short answer

Amazon has no universal restock schedule. Each seller sets their own reorder point and lead time, so restock timing is decided at the SKU level, not company-wide. Returned items go back into stock only if they pass Amazon's inspection as sellable; damaged or opened units usually do not.

There's no company-wide restock date — here's why

Most listings on Amazon are sold by individual sellers, either fulfilled by Amazon (FBA) or by the seller directly (FBM). A smaller share is sold by Amazon itself as first-party retail. Each of these has its own supply chain, its own supplier lead time, and its own decision about when to reorder. So when someone asks when Amazon restocks their items, the honest answer is: that decision belongs to whichever seller owns the listing, not to Amazon as a company.

Amazon's own customer service page addresses this from the shopper side with a feature called Availability Alerts — the "Alert Me" box on an out-of-stock product page. Signing up gets you an email when the item becomes available again. It does not reserve a unit for you, and Amazon is explicit that the price in that email can change by the time you act on it. It's a notification tool, not a restock calendar.

If you're a seller asking the same question about your own catalog, the answer is more useful but requires more work: it depends on your reorder point, your lead time, and how much of your restock limit you have left for that storage type.

How sellers actually decide when to reorder

Every FBA restock decision comes down to one calculation: daily sales velocity multiplied by lead time, plus a safety stock buffer for demand swings and shipping delays. Say a product sells 20 units a day, the supplier lead time is 30 days including manufacturing and ocean freight, and the seller carries a 10-day safety buffer. The reorder point is 20 x (30 + 10) = 800 units. When on-hand inventory hits 800 units, that's the trigger to place the next purchase order — not a date on a calendar, a stock level.

This is why two sellers of near-identical products can have completely different restock timing. One with a 15-day domestic lead time reorders far later, and far more often, than one sourcing from overseas with a 60-day lead time. The reorder point, not the calendar, is the real answer to "when."

The common mistake here is calculating the reorder point once and never touching it again. Velocity changes after a promotion, a competitor stockout, or a seasonal shift, and a reorder point built on stale sales data either triggers too late — and you go out of stock — or too early, tying up cash in units you didn't need yet.

Does Amazon restock returned items?

Sometimes, and it depends entirely on the return's condition. When a customer sends an item back to an FBA warehouse, Amazon inspects and grades it before deciding what happens next.

  • Unopened, undamaged, unexpired: re-inspected, relabeled if needed, and returned to sellable FBA inventory — often within days.
  • Opened but functional, and the category allows it: may be repackaged and returned to stock, or moved to unsellable depending on Amazon's category rules.
  • Damaged, missing parts, or used beyond resale condition: moved to unsellable inventory. The seller then chooses removal, liquidation, or donation — Amazon does not put it back on the shelf.
  • Recalled, expired, or hazmat-flagged units: disposed of per policy, no seller choice involved.

Across the $500M+ in Amazon spend Full Circle has managed for 100+ brands, the return-to-stock decision is one of the most under-tracked numbers in a seller's account — it's easy to see the refund on a P&L and never check whether the unit actually made it back to sellable stock, got written off, or is sitting in unsellable inventory racking up long-term storage fees.

When the restock timing is wrong, or the fix didn't work

If a reorder point trigger fires and the unit still runs out, check three things in order: whether the lead time you used is still accurate (supplier lead times creep up during peak season and rarely creep back down on their own), whether your restock limit for that storage type has room, and whether the sales velocity feeding the calculation is from before or after whatever caused the spike.

Restock limits are Amazon's separate cap on how much inventory you can hold in FBA at all, by marketplace and storage type, tied partly to your Inventory Performance Index. A perfectly calculated reorder point is useless if your restock limit won't let the shipment in. This is genuinely a different problem from reorder timing, and it's the one that forecasting tools like SoStocked are built to track — they watch your restock limit utilization and flag when you're close to the ceiling. That's a real, narrow, useful job, and it's worth having if restock limits are your main headache.

One mistake we've made ourselves: setting a safety stock buffer as a flat number of days rather than scaling it to how variable that specific supplier's lead time actually is. It works fine until the supplier's lead time doubles during a peak-season freight crunch, and a flat 10-day buffer that was fine in March isn't enough in October.

Common questions

Does Amazon restock items automatically?

No. Third-party sellers place their own purchase orders based on their own reorder point and lead time. Amazon's first-party retail listings follow their own internal replenishment process, which isn't visible to sellers or shoppers. There is no single company-wide restocking schedule.

How do I find out when an out-of-stock item will be back?

If the product page shows an "Alert Me" box, you can sign up with your email and Amazon will notify you when it's available again. That doesn't reserve a unit or guarantee a date — it's a notification, and the price you see in the email can change before you check out.

Does Amazon restock returned items?

Only if the return passes inspection as sellable. Unopened, undamaged returns generally go back into stock quickly. Opened, damaged, or used returns are usually routed to unsellable inventory, where the seller decides whether to remove, liquidate, or donate them.

Why did my restock limit drop even though I still have inventory coming?

Restock limits are set by marketplace and storage type and shift with your Inventory Performance Index and overall FBA capacity, independent of any single shipment. A dropped limit is a separate problem from a mistimed reorder point, and it's worth checking Seller Central's inventory performance dashboard directly rather than assuming your forecast was wrong.

What's the actual difference between a stockout and a restock limit problem?

A stockout means you ran out of units to sell — a reorder point or lead-time problem. A restock limit problem means you have units ready to ship but Amazon's storage cap for that type won't let them in yet. They look similar from the storefront but need different fixes.
