How the two actually relate

Reorder level (also called reorder point, or ROP) is the number of units left on the shelf at which you place a new order. Safety stock is the cushion built into that number to cover the gap between what you expected to sell and what you actually sell before the next shipment lands. They're not two competing metrics — safety stock is one of the three inputs to reorder level, alongside average daily sales and lead time.

Skip safety stock and your reorder level only works if demand and lead time are perfectly steady, which they never are. A supplier ships two days late, or a listing has a good week, and a reorder level built on averages alone runs out before the next shipment arrives. Safety stock is what keeps that variance from turning into a stockout.

The reorder level formula with safety stock

The standard formula: Reorder level = (average daily sales × lead time) + safety stock.

Say a mid-velocity SKU sells an average of 40 units a day. Supplier lead time is 21 days. On Amazon, lead time isn't just the supplier's quoted number — it's supplier lead time plus inbound transit plus FBA receiving lag before units actually go sellable. Add a realistic 4 days for transit and receiving and total lead time is 25 days, not 21.

If safety stock for this SKU works out to 74 units (worked below), the reorder level is: (40 × 25) + 74 = 1,074 units. That's the count at which a new PO should go out — not when inventory hits zero.

Two ways to calculate safety stock — they don't give you the same number

There are two methods sellers actually use, and they can land far apart.

  • Max-minus-average method: (max daily sales × max lead time) − (average daily sales × average lead time). Using max daily sales of 55, max lead time of 30 days, average daily sales of 40, and average lead time of 25 days: (55 × 30) − (40 × 25) = 1,650 − 1,000 = 650 units of safety stock. It's simple and it assumes your worst demand day and your worst lead-time delay happen at the same time — which is rare, so it tends to over-buffer.
  • Service-level (Z-score) method: Z × standard deviation of daily sales × √(lead time). Using a standard deviation of 9 units, a 95% service level (Z = 1.65), and a 25-day lead time: 1.65 × 9 × √25 = 1.65 × 9 × 5 = 74 units. It needs a standard deviation, which most sellers don't have sitting in a spreadsheet, but it prices the buffer to an actual service level instead of a worst-case coincidence.

Neither method is wrong. The gap between 650 and 74 units for the same SKU is the real cost of picking one over the other — it's cash sitting in a warehouse either way, so the choice matters more than most guides let on.

When the number is wrong — and what to check first

If you're still stocking out despite a formula that looks correct, the math usually isn't the problem. The inputs are. Across the $500M-plus in Amazon spend we've managed for 100-plus brands, the single most common error isn't the calculation — it's the lead time input. Sellers use the supplier's quoted number and leave out inbound transit and FBA receiving lag, which quietly adds days and turns a clean reorder point into a stockout three weeks later.

Second thing to check: was the spike in demand that triggered the panic actually a new baseline, or a one-week anomaly? We've gotten this wrong ourselves — recalculating safety stock upward after a promotional bump, before confirming the bump wasn't temporary, which tied up cash in stock the SKU didn't need for the next two months.

Third: if the spike traces back to a PPC launch, a deal, or a coupon that pulled velocity up temporarily, that's a demand-side signal from the ad account, not a new baseline for inventory planning — that conversation belongs with whoever runs the ads, not the reorder formula.

Common questions

What is safety stock level?

Safety stock level is the extra inventory held above what average demand and average lead time require, sized to absorb demand spikes or supplier delays without running out. It's expressed as a unit count for a given SKU, and it changes if demand variability or lead time changes.

What's the reorder level formula with safety stock?

Reorder level = (average daily sales × lead time) + safety stock. On Amazon, lead time needs to include inbound transit and FBA receiving lag, not just the supplier's quoted production and shipping time — that's the input most sellers get wrong first.

How much safety stock should I actually hold?

It depends on the service level you're targeting, not a fixed percentage. A 95% service level uses a Z-score of roughly 1.65; a 99% target uses roughly 2.33. Higher service levels mean more safety stock and more cash tied up, so the target should match how much a stockout actually costs that SKU.

Does the reorder level change over time?

Yes. It's built from averages and variability that shift with seasonality, promotions, and supplier performance. Recalculating monthly, or right after a lead-time change or a demand shift you're confident is real, keeps the number from going stale.

Why do I still stock out even with a correct-looking reorder point?

Usually one of three things: lead time was defined too narrowly and left out receiving lag, the demand spike behind the stockout was ad-driven rather than a new baseline, or the PO that should have triggered at the reorder level was placed late. Check the input before touching the formula.
