What Amazon Business Restock Actually Is

Amazon Business Restock is a real product, but it is not for third-party sellers. It's Amazon's own replenishment service for corporate buyers — companies that use Amazon Business to keep breakrooms, office supplies, PPE and IT accessories stocked at physical worksites.

Two things worth knowing before you contact a rep. First, it's offered only in select U.S. cities, and availability is confirmed through an Account Executive, not a signup page. Second, there's no published price — Amazon states subscription cost depends on the number of sites serviced, so no number exists until you're on a call.

If you run a facilities or procurement team buying office and MRO supplies, this is worth exploring. If you sell products on Amazon and are trying to restock your own inventory, it's the wrong product entirely — it does not touch your FBA account.

How Amazon Business Restock Works

It comes in three forms, each suited to a different kind of item:

  • Managed Inventory: Amazon technicians visit a stockroom, monitor stock levels and reorder for you. It suits everyday consumables used across a site.
  • Vending: a machine controls distribution and reorders automatically when levels drop. It suits items you want issued in a controlled way, such as PPE.
  • Lockers: for higher-value items like laptops or safety radios, checked in and out by badge so you can see who has what.

In every form the idea is the same: the reorder trigger moves from a person remembering to check the shelf to a level being monitored. The buyer still orders through Amazon Business; the service takes over the watching and the replenishing.

Restocking Across Multiple Business Locations

A common question is how Amazon Business Restock handles several workspaces or business locations. Amazon's stated structure is per site: the subscription cost depends on the number of sites serviced, and availability depends on your city. So a multi-location buyer should not expect one price or one answer for every building.

Questions to put to your Account Executive before you commit:

  • Which of our locations are in a covered city today, and which are not?
  • Can different sites use different forms (managed inventory in one, lockers in another)?
  • How is the subscription calculated as we add or remove a site?
  • Who sets the stock levels that trigger a reorder at each site, and how often are they reviewed?
  • How do orders and invoices appear in our Amazon Business account for each location?

Get the answers in writing. They decide whether the service fits a spread-out footprint better than a single-site one.

If You're a Seller: The Reorder-Point Math Instead

If you searched this term because you sell on Amazon and need to know when to reorder your own inventory, the question isn't about a subscription service. It's about timing a purchase order against real sales velocity and real lead time.

Getting it wrong in either direction costs money. Reorder too late and you stock out, which often means losing organic rank mid-campaign. Reorder too early or too much, and the cash sits in a warehouse as a slow-moving SKU while you pay long-term storage and aged-inventory surcharges.

The formula: Reorder point = (average daily sales × lead time in days) + (average daily sales × safety stock in days). Worked example: a SKU selling 40 units a day, with a 35-day lead time and 15 days of safety stock. That is (40 × 35) + (40 × 15) = 1,400 + 600 = 2,000 units. Place the next purchase order when sellable stock hits 2,000 units, not when a dashboard flags 'low stock' — by then you're often already inside the lead-time window.

The Common Mistake — Including Ones We've Made

The mistake we see most often, and one we've made ourselves, is treating lead time as a fixed number instead of a range. A SKU that normally takes 35 days door-to-door can take 50 during a freight rate spike, a factory holiday, or port congestion — and if the reorder point was calculated on the 35-day version, the safety stock buffer gets eaten before the new inventory lands.

Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon revenue across 100+ brands, and the recurring failure across that book isn't bad math — it's stale math. A reorder point calculated correctly in January is often wrong by Q3, because sales velocity changed, freight changed, or a supplier changed terms. The fix is revisiting the inputs on a set cadence, especially lead time, which is the input sellers most often assume is stable and least often check.

When the Signal Is Wrong: Troubleshooting

Sometimes you do the math and the answer is still bad news. A few situations worth naming directly:

  • The reorder point fires but the PO doesn't land in time anyway. That usually means lead time was underestimated, not that the formula failed. Rebuild it from your actual last three shipments, not the supplier's quoted lead time.
  • You already have a 'reorder alert' turned on somewhere — in Seller Central, a spreadsheet macro, a 3PL dashboard — and it's still missing stockouts. A flat 30-day threshold applied to every SKU will always be wrong for both your fastest and slowest movers. Set it per SKU.
  • The fix didn't work and you stocked out again. Before changing the formula again, check whether the stockout was demand-side — a promotion or a competitor's own stockout driving a spike — rather than supply-side. Different problem, different fix.

How does Amazon Business Restock work?
