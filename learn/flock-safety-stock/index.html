Is Flock Safety Publicly Traded? The Short Answer

No. Flock Safety is a privately held company founded in 2017 and based in Atlanta, making license plate readers, video cameras, drones and related public-safety software. Private companies don't get a ticker symbol — that's a designation exchanges assign only to companies that have filed an S-1 and listed publicly. Flock Safety hasn't done either.

Because there's no listing, there's no continuously quoted market price either. What exists instead is a private secondary market: a small number of platforms that match people who hold Flock Safety shares (usually employees or early investors) with people who want to buy them. Nasdaq Private Market and Hiive are the two currently doing this for Flock Safety.

How People Actually Buy or Sell Flock Safety Shares

Access is restricted on both sides of the trade. To buy, you generally need to qualify as an accredited investor or institutional buyer — that's a legal status based on income, net worth, or professional certification, not just having money to spend. To sell, you typically need to already hold shares as an employee, ex-employee, or investor, and the company itself has to approve the transfer.

That last part matters more than it sounds. Flock Safety, like most private companies, holds a right of first refusal on its own stock. Even if you find a willing buyer at an agreed price on Hiive or Nasdaq Private Market, the sale isn't final until the company signs off. If you're an employee sitting on shares and hoping to cash out on your own timeline, that approval step — not the marketplace — is usually the actual bottleneck.

  • Can't buy today: if you're not accredited, no platform gets you around that. It's a regulatory requirement, not a platform limitation.
  • Selling is stalled: check whether it's the marketplace or the company's approval that's holding things up — they're different problems with different fixes.

Why the Price You See Depends on Which Site You're On

Here's a real snapshot from the same week: Nasdaq Private Market showed Flock Safety at $12.11 a share. Hiive showed $10.34. Neither is wrong exactly — they're both estimates, built from different formulas, and neither is a live trade you could execute at that instant.

Look at the spread on Nasdaq Private Market's own page: highest bid $260.80, lowest offer $188.50, last trade $234.00. That's a gap of more than $70 between what a buyer was willing to pay and what a seller was asking — roughly 30% of the last trade price. On Hiive's page, the bid and ask fields showed placeholders with only a handful of live orders behind them. A single headline number is standing in for a wide, thin, and sometimes empty market.

The practical takeaway: if you're using either number to decide what your own shares are worth, treat it as a starting range, not a price. The real number is whatever a specific buyer agrees to pay after the company signs off — and that can land anywhere inside that spread, or outside it.

The Mistake Most People Make Reading These Numbers

The mistake is reading a private-market "price" the way you'd read a Nasdaq quote — as a single, current, tradeable fact. It isn't. Nasdaq Private Market's own disclosure describes its figure as a model-derived estimate based on market activity and proprietary data. Hiive describes its own figure as a time-decayed, volume-weighted blend of confirmed trades and bid/ask midpoints. Both are careful to say, in their own fine print, that the number is an approximation — not a quote you can act on without more digging.

Flock Safety's IPO: What's Actually Been Announced

Nothing, publicly. Flock Safety has not filed the paperwork (an S-1) that precedes a public listing, and none of the sources tracking the company report a set IPO date or price. Its most recent disclosed funding is a Series H that closed in March 2025; Hiive's data shows $275M raised in that round as part of $961.16M raised across the company's full financing history, with backers including Tiger Global Management, Meritech Capital Partners, and Seven Seven Six.

Until an IPO happens, three things stay true: there's no ticker symbol, there's no way to buy shares through an ordinary brokerage account, and any price you see quoted is a marketplace estimate rather than a market price. If you're specifically watching for the IPO itself, the reliable signal is a Form S-1 filing with the SEC or an official announcement from Flock Safety — not a change in the number on a secondary-market dashboard.

Common questions

What is Flock Safety's stock ticker symbol?

There isn't one. Ticker symbols are assigned to companies listed on public exchanges. Flock Safety is private and has never listed, so it has no ticker — it's identified only by its company name.

Can I buy Flock Safety stock through a regular brokerage account like Fidelity or Robinhood?

No. Flock Safety shares aren't available on any public exchange or standard brokerage. The only route is a private secondary marketplace such as Nasdaq Private Market or Hiive, and only if you qualify as an accredited investor.

Has Flock Safety filed for an IPO?

Not as of the most recent public information. No S-1 filing or IPO date has been announced. Its last disclosed raise was a Series H round that closed in March 2025.

Why do Nasdaq Private Market and Hiive show different prices for Flock Safety?

They calculate the number differently. Both are estimates built from limited trades and bid/ask data rather than live market quotes, and their own disclosures say so directly — treat either figure as a range, not a fixed price.

I'm a Flock Safety employee with shares. Why is my sale stuck?

Most likely it's the company's approval step, not the marketplace. Private companies typically hold a right of first refusal, meaning even an agreed sale on Hiive or Nasdaq Private Market needs Flock Safety's sign-off before it settles.
