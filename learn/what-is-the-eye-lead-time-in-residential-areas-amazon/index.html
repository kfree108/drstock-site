What eye lead time actually measures

Eye lead time is the number of seconds ahead of your vehicle that your eyes are actually scanning — not the distance you're following the car in front of you. It comes out of commercial fleet driver training, most directly the Smith System and similar defensive-driving programs, which teach it as one of the core habits alongside keeping your eyes moving and getting the big picture. Amazon's DSP (Delivery Service Partner) safety programs use the same idea, and it shows up in driver coaching and in-vehicle camera systems that flag short look-ahead moments the same way they flag hard braking or phone use.

It's easy to confuse with the two-second following-distance rule, but they measure different things. Following distance is the gap between you and the vehicle ahead. Eye lead time is about where your attention is pointed — a driver can have a perfect following gap and still score poorly on eye lead if their eyes are locked on the bumper in front of them instead of scanning the block ahead for a kid, a dog, or a car door about to open.

Why Amazon singles out residential areas

Amazon calls out residential areas specifically because the hazard count per block goes up even though speed goes down. On a residential street a driver deals with driveways that hide sightlines, cars parked close to intersections, kids and pets that appear without warning, mail carriers and other delivery vans stopping and reversing, and trash or recycling bins narrowing the lane on collection day. Almost none of that shows up on a highway.

Amazon does not publish a single number that applies to every DSP, every region, and every route, and we're not going to invent one here. In-vehicle camera systems score driving behavior in real time, and the specific eye lead time threshold your coach references — if you're being told a number — comes from your DSP's own safety program and dispatch guidance. That's the number to follow, not any figure printed on a general information page.

The math behind eye lead time — a worked example

The concept becomes concrete once you turn it into distance instead of seconds, because distance is what your eyes actually have to cover. Speed in miles per hour converts to feet per second by multiplying by roughly 1.47. A van doing 25 mph is covering about 37 feet every second, so a 4-second eye lead means scanning roughly 147 feet ahead — about half a residential block.

If your eye lead time gets flagged, what to actually do

If a coaching report or scorecard flags short eye lead time, the fix isn't to argue with the camera. Start by picking a fixed point on the road well before you reach it — a stop sign, a mailbox, the end of a driveway — and naming it out loud as you drive. That forces your eyes to travel forward instead of settling on the hood or the vehicle ahead, which is the single most common cause of a low score.

Second, widen your side-to-side scan, not just your forward one. Residential hazards come from driveways and gaps between parked cars as often as they come from straight ahead, and a driver who's technically looking far enough down the road but never checking the gaps on either side will still get flagged, and rightly so.

If you've genuinely corrected the habit and the score hasn't moved, ask your DSP safety team what the camera system is actually detecting. Some systems infer eye lead from vehicle speed and braking patterns rather than eye tracking, and a mechanical issue — worn brakes, a heavier load — can produce the same flag as a genuine scanning problem.

The most common mistake, including one we've watched experienced drivers make

The most common mistake is treating eye lead time as a highway skill that residential streets don't really need, because speeds are lower and it feels safer. That's backwards. Lower speed buys you more reaction time per hazard, but residential blocks pack in more hazards per hundred feet than almost any other road type, which cancels the advantage out.

The second mistake, and one we've watched experienced fleet coaches make, is over-indexing on the scorecard number itself instead of the habit behind it. A driver can learn to beat a camera-based score without actually improving how far ahead they're looking, and that shows up the first week the system or the route changes. The habit is the thing worth building, not the number on this week's report.

Common questions

Is eye lead time an official Amazon term?

No. It's a defensive-driving concept — most directly from the Smith System — that predates Amazon and is used across commercial fleets. Amazon's DSP safety programs have adopted the term and the underlying habit, but it isn't unique to Amazon; you'll hear the same phrase from trucking and bus-driver trainers.

What eye lead time does Amazon require in residential areas?

There's no single published figure that applies everywhere. The specific threshold a driver is coached against comes from their DSP's own safety program and camera system, and it can vary by region and route type. If your coach or app cites a number, that's the one that applies to you.

How is eye lead time actually measured on a delivery route?

Most DSP fleets use in-vehicle camera systems that infer scanning behavior from where the driver's head and eyes are pointed, combined with vehicle telemetry like speed, braking, and following distance. A short eye lead event is usually flagged alongside other safety events such as hard braking or following too close.

What's the difference between eye lead time and following distance?

Following distance is the gap, in seconds, between your vehicle and the one ahead of you. Eye lead time is where your eyes are pointed, independent of that gap — you can have a safe following distance and still have poor eye lead time if you're not scanning far enough ahead for hazards outside your own lane.

Can a low eye lead time score affect a driver's standing with their DSP?

It can, since it's typically bundled into a broader safety scorecard that DSPs and Amazon both review. The exact consequences — coaching, retraining, or something more serious — depend on the DSP's own policy, not on anything published by Amazon at a general level, so the honest answer is to ask your specific DSP safety contact.
