What Amazon Inventory Tracking Actually Covers

Most pages on this topic jump straight to software. The useful starting point is simpler: inventory tracking is knowing where every unit of a SKU is at this moment, and how long that stock will last.

For an FBA seller, a SKU is usually split across five places at once:

  • Sellable: in a fulfillment center and available to buy.
  • Inbound: shipped to Amazon but not yet checked in. It cannot sell until it is received.
  • Reserved: held for open orders or being moved between fulfillment centers.
  • Unfulfillable: damaged, expired or otherwise not sellable. It still sits on your books until you remove it or Amazon reimburses it.
  • Upstream: at your supplier, in production, on the water or in a 3PL. Amazon cannot see this stock at all.

Seller Central's inventory pages cover the first four. The fifth lives in your own records, and it is the one that decides whether you stock out in eight weeks. Tracking that only looks at the Amazon dashboard is tracking half the pipeline.

The Numbers to Check Every Week

You do not need thirty metrics. Four, checked per SKU rather than for the account as a whole, catch most problems early:

  • Average daily sales (velocity): units sold per day over a recent window, such as the last 30 days. Use actual sales, not the forecast.
  • Days of cover: sellable plus inbound units divided by daily sales. This is the number that tells you how long you have.
  • Lead time: the real door-to-door time from purchase order to FBA check-in, taken from your last few shipments rather than the supplier's quote.
  • Unfulfillable and aged units: stock that is costing you storage and not selling.

The comparison that matters is days of cover against lead time plus a safety buffer. If cover is shorter than that, the purchase order is already late, whatever a dashboard colour says.

A Worked Example: Days of Cover

These are illustrative numbers, not a client result. Say a SKU sells 40 units a day. Seller Central shows 1,200 sellable units and 800 inbound.

  • Sellable plus inbound: 1,200 + 800 = 2,000 units.
  • Days of cover: 2,000 ÷ 40 = 50 days.
  • Lead time from your last three shipments: 35 days. Safety buffer: 15 days. Needed: 35 + 15 = 50 days.

Cover equals the need exactly, so the next purchase order should go out now. Notice what a sellable-only view would have said: 1,200 ÷ 40 = 30 days, which looks like an emergency even though 800 units are on the way. And the reverse mistake is just as common: counting inbound units as if they were sellable, when a check-in delay can leave them out of reach for weeks.

Run the same sum on every SKU. The one that surprises you is rarely your top seller, which gets watched anyway. It is the mid-sized SKU that quietly crossed its reorder point while nobody was looking.

Spreadsheet, Seller Central or Software?

All three work at the right size. Each has a point where it stops being enough.

  • Seller Central alone suits a handful of SKUs with short, predictable lead times. It is free and always current, but it shows only what is inside Amazon. It has no view of upstream stock, and you build the days-of-cover maths yourself.
  • A spreadsheet suits small catalogues and gives you full control. It breaks when the data is pasted in by hand, when one person owns it, or when more than one marketplace or warehouse is involved. Stale cells are the usual failure.
  • Dedicated inventory software suits catalogues large enough that checking each SKU weekly is a job in itself, or sellers with several suppliers, marketplaces or 3PLs. Many tools exist, and several are good. Compare how each handles upstream stock, per-SKU lead times and alerts before price.

A fair rule of thumb: move up a level when the tracking itself starts causing late purchase orders, not before.

What Tracking Alone Does Not Catch

Knowing your counts is necessary, but several costly problems only show up when you reconcile the counts against Amazon's records and fees:

  • Lost or damaged units that never appear as a reimbursement. If the units you sent do not match the units Amazon received or sold, someone has to notice.
  • Storage and aged-inventory charges building on slow SKUs, which are cheaper to act on early than after the charge posts.
  • Fee errors from wrong dimensions or weights on a listing.
  • Cash tied up in SKUs with several months of cover while a faster SKU runs short.

This is where the common failure sits. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon revenue across 100+ brands, and the recurring problem is rarely that a seller has no tracking. It is stale tracking: lead times assumed rather than measured, and counts checked monthly when the SKU turns weekly. The fix is a set cadence and the same four inputs, reviewed on purpose.

A Simple Weekly Routine

If you do only one thing from this page, make it this checklist, run once a week:

  • Pull sellable, inbound and reserved units for every active SKU.
  • Add your upstream stock from supplier and freight records.
  • Recalculate daily sales from the last 30 days.
  • Work out days of cover and compare it to lead time plus buffer.
  • Flag every SKU that is short, and every SKU with far more cover than it needs.
  • Check for unfulfillable units and units that were sent but never received.
  • Update lead times from your three most recent shipments.

Done by hand, this takes time proportional to your SKU count. That is the honest test for whether you need a tool: if the routine is sound but you keep skipping it, automate the routine rather than inventing a new one.

Common questions

How do I track my inventory on Amazon?

Start with the Manage Inventory and inbound shipment pages in Seller Central for sellable, inbound, reserved and unfulfillable units. Then add the stock Amazon cannot see, at your supplier or in transit, and calculate days of cover for each SKU.

What is the difference between sellable and inbound inventory?

Sellable units are checked in and available to buy. Inbound units have been shipped to Amazon but not received, so they cannot sell yet. Count them separately, and add them together only when working out days of cover.

How do I calculate days of cover for an FBA SKU?

Divide sellable plus inbound units by average daily sales. If a SKU sells 40 a day and you hold 2,000 units in total, that is 50 days. Compare the result to your real lead time plus a safety buffer.

Do I need inventory software to track Amazon stock?

Not at first. Seller Central plus a spreadsheet is enough for a small catalogue. Software starts to pay off when weekly checks become too slow, or when you manage several suppliers, marketplaces or warehouses.

How often should I check Amazon inventory levels?

Weekly per SKU is a sensible minimum, and more often for fast sellers or during promotions. Lead times and sales speed change, so recalculate the inputs each time rather than reusing old ones.
