The problem / 01

Nobody is watching where the money actually goes.

Every brand on Amazon has a person who watches ad spend, because ad spend arrives as a bill. Inventory does not send you a bill. It quietly takes your cash, sits on it, charges you rent, and occasionally loses some of it — and none of that shows up as a line item you can argue with. These are the places it leaks. Dr. Stock's whole job is to find them, price them against your real numbers, and do something about them before the next cycle.

Leak 01

Running out

A stockout does not just cost you the sales you did not make that week. It costs you the rank you spent months buying, the review velocity, and the ad history — and you pay to win all of it back at a higher price than holding the units would have cost.
What he does: forecasts demand per SKU against real lead time and its variability, not a flat 30-day average, and tells you the date the purchase order has to leave the supplier.
Leak 02

Cash trapped in the wrong SKUs

Most catalogs have a handful of products that turn and a long tail that does not. The tail is not free — it is your working capital, converted into cardboard, sitting still. That is money you cannot spend on the products that actually sell.
What he does: ranks every SKU by how fast the cash comes back, flags where capital is parked, and puts a plan on the slow units instead of letting them age quietly.
Leak 03

Storage and aged-inventory surcharges

Amazon charges monthly storage, and charges more for units that have been sitting a long time. Those charges escalate on a schedule, which means the cost of a bad ordering decision keeps growing after the decision is already made.
What he does: watches the age bands on every SKU and raises the removal, liquidation or discount decision before the unit crosses into the next surcharge tier, not after.
Leak 04

Fee errors on your own catalog

FBA fees are calculated from the dimensions and weight Amazon has on file for each SKU. Those measurements are sometimes wrong, and a size-tier or dimensional-weight misclassification is a common, checkable error that quietly charges you on every single unit you ship.
What he does: reconciles the fee charged against the measurements on file, SKU by SKU, and where the numbers disagree he assembles the evidence and requests a re-measure through Amazon's own process.
Leak 05

Shipments that don't arrive whole

What you shipped and what Amazon booked in are not always the same number. Units go missing, get damaged in the network, or get received short — and the claim window does not stay open forever.
What he does: reconciles every shipment against what was received, and files documented discrepancies through Amazon's own case process, inside Amazon's stated windows.
Leak 06

Returns and unfulfillable units

Returned units come back in three states: sellable, unsellable, and never came back at all. Each one has a different correct action, and doing nothing is the expensive default because the unit keeps accruing storage while you decide.
What he does: tracks return reason and disposition per SKU, separates a product problem from a listing problem, and clears the unfulfillable pile deliberately.

We are deliberately not putting a dollar figure on any of these. The honest answer is that the size depends entirely on your catalog, your margins and your lead times — and any vendor quoting you a recovery number before they have seen your account is guessing. The first thing the demo does is measure yours.

How he thinks / 03

Fable 5, pointed at your whole catalog.

The model is the engine. A decade of operating accounts is the fuel. Together they produce something no spreadsheet and no reorder alert can: a specific written plan for every SKU you sell — what to order, when, what to do with what you already own, and what to go and claim back.

01

Read everything

Sales velocity, inbound shipments, on-hand and reserved units, return dispositions, fee schedules, storage age bands and settlement data — reconciled against each other, not read one report at a time.
02

Model demand per SKU

Seasonality, promotional lift, ad pressure and trend, at the SKU level. A parent's average is not a child's forecast, and one number for the catalog is how brands end up long on the wrong colourway.
03

Model the supply side honestly

Production time, transit, customs, receive time at the fulfilment centre — and the variance on each. The reorder date comes from the whole chain, not from a lead time somebody typed in once.
04

Price the decision

Every recommendation is weighed in cash: what it ties up, what it frees, what it costs to hold, and what it costs to be wrong in each direction.
05

Propose or act

Reorder quantities and dates, removals, liquidations, fee disputes, shipment claims. Either executed inside your guardrails or queued for your approval. You decide which.
06

Log every reason

Each decision is written down with the evidence behind it and the assumption it rests on. If Dr. Stock cannot explain why he wants 900 units instead of 600, he does not ask for 900.

Your call / 05

You choose how much he does on his own.

"Autonomous" is not one setting, and it should never be the vendor's decision — least of all when the decisions spend your money on stock. Dr. Stock runs at whichever of these three levels you pick, and you can change it any time, without renegotiating anything. Some brands sit at Level 1 through their first season, read the log, and move up. Some never do. Both are fine.

Level 01

Full human approval

Dr. Stock does all the analysis and proposes every decision. Nothing is ordered, removed or filed without a human clicking approve.
  • Every reorder, removal, liquidation, fee dispute and claim arrives as a recommendation card
  • Each card carries the evidence, the assumption it rests on, and what happens if that assumption breaks
  • Approve, reject, or approve in bulk — one click each
  • Rejections are read back into the model, so he stops proposing what you keep refusing

Level 02

Supervised autonomy

The routine, low-risk, well-evidenced work runs on its own inside guardrails you set. Anything above your threshold queues for approval.
  • Runs on its own: shipment reconciliation, documented claims, fee re-measure requests, routine removals of unfulfillable units
  • Queues for you: purchase orders, anything above your value threshold, new SKUs, first orders with a new supplier
  • You set the band — order values, removal thresholds, which SKUs are untouchable
  • Daily digest of what ran automatically, with the same evidence attached
The fee dispute aboveA documented, checkable mismatch — filed automatically, logged, nothing irreversible.
Level 03

Fully autonomous

Dr. Stock runs the supply chain inside agreed guardrails. You review the log and the outcomes rather than each decision.
  • Every decision is still written down with its reasoning and its evidence
  • Every decision still names the assumption it depends on and what happens if it breaks
  • Guardrails are yours: capital ceilings, maximum order values, cover targets, protected SKUs, approved suppliers
  • Weekly written supply chain update; drop back to Level 1 at any moment

What does not change between levels

The same evidence trail

Level 3 is not a quieter product. Every decision produces the identical written record — you are choosing whether to read it before or after.

The same stated assumptions

Nothing is executed without naming what it depends on. Autonomy raises the speed, not the confidence.

The same hard limits

The list below is off-limits at every level, including Level 3. Full autonomy does not mean unlimited authority over your bank account.

The same switch, both ways

Move up when you trust it, drop back the week your supplier gets interesting. Month-to-month, so there is nothing to renegotiate.

Honesty / 07

What he does alone — and what he never does.

Everyone in this category says "autonomous" and nobody defines it. So here is ours, in public. The right-hand column applies at every approval level, including Level 3 — these are not permissions you can switch on.

Runs unattended

  • Forecasts demand and recalculates days of cover on every SKU, daily

  • Clears unfulfillable units and flags stranded inventory

Always needs a human

  • Launching a new product or entering a new category
  • Pricing and promotional decisions
  • Anything depending on information he cannot see — a factory closure, a quality problem, a container you already know is late
  • Writing off inventory, and any decision with a tax or accounting consequence
  • Deciding what level of stockout risk your business is willing to carry

Questions / 11

The things people actually ask.

What happens in the first 30 days?
Dr. Stock reads the catalog, models demand and lead times per SKU, reconciles your fees and shipments, and tells you where your cash currently is. You get the written plan and the daily loop from day one. Most brands start at Level 1 so they can read every recommendation before it does anything. You pay nothing. If it has not earned the second month by day 30, we shake hands and part ways.
What does it cost after that?
We quote it on the call, in writing, once we have seen the catalog. We are deliberately not putting a number on this page for a product whose right shape depends on how many SKUs you carry and where the money is going. There is no onboarding fee, no contract, and Orbit is included at no additional charge.
Does he place purchase orders with my supplier?
Only if you explicitly turn that on, and only inside limits you set. By default he produces the reorder recommendation — quantity, the date it has to leave the supplier, and the assumptions behind it — and a human sends it. Money leaving your bank account is a human decision unless you decide otherwise.
How does the fee and claim work actually work?
Two reconciliations. He compares what you shipped against what Amazon received, and he compares the fees charged against the dimensions and weights Amazon has on file for each SKU. Where there is a documented, checkable disagreement, he assembles the evidence and files through Amazon's own case process, inside Amazon's stated windows. We do not promise a recovery figure, because nobody honestly can — the correct answer is whatever the reconciliation returns.
Can I keep approval on everything?
Yes — that is Level 1, and plenty of accounts stay there through their first season. Dr. Stock still does all the analysis and writes every recommendation; he just waits for your click. You can move up later, or move back down, at any time and without renegotiating your agreement.
If I go fully autonomous, what stops a bad decision?
Three things. Guardrails you set before he starts — capital ceilings, maximum order values, cover targets, protected SKUs, approved suppliers. An assumption named on every single decision, so a plan built on a lead time that turns out to be wrong is re-run rather than defended. And the hard limits in the ladder above, which apply at every level: supplier choice, pricing, new launches, write-offs and anything he cannot see always come to a human.
Do you replace my 3PL or my ops person?
No. Dr. Stock decides what to order, when, and what to do with what you already own. Your 3PL still moves boxes and your ops person still handles the relationships and the exceptions — the part of the job that actually needs a human. What changes is that the decision arrives already worked out, every day, on every SKU, instead of at the end of a spreadsheet evening.
Is Orbit really included?
Yes. The full suite — inventory, finance, sales, advertising, ASIN profitability, the BSR, buy box, price and fee trackers, and the Dr Stock Console itself — comes with the service at no additional charge. There is no separate software line on your invoice and no tier you have to buy up into to see your own numbers.
Is this just a reorder alert with a marketing name?
No. A reorder point fires when a number crosses a line you configured. Fable 5 models demand and supply per SKU against your real economics, prices each option in cash, then writes the decision down with the assumption it rests on — so you can audit the thinking, not just the alert.
Can I run Dr. Stock and Dr. PPC together?
Yes, and it is the combination we would pick. Ads and inventory are the same decision seen from two ends — there is no point buying rank for a product that is three weeks from going out of stock, and no point holding stock nobody is driving traffic to. Both live in the same Orbit account and read the same numbers.
