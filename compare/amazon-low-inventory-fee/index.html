What the low inventory fee is, and why Amazon charges it

Amazon runs its fulfilment network on the assumption that popular products are in stock. When a fast seller has only a few days of units left, Amazon has to split shipments, reroute orders or lose the sale. The low-inventory-level fee is how Amazon prices that cost back to the seller.

In plain terms: the fee is about cover, not about how many units you hold. A seller with 500 units can be charged if those units sell in a week. A seller with 50 units is fine if they sell in two months. That is why the fee surprises people who feel they have plenty of stock.

It is charged per unit shipped, so it scales with your volume on the affected SKUs. It sits alongside the monthly storage fee, the aged inventory surcharge and the storage utilization surcharge, but it is the opposite problem: those punish holding too much, this one punishes holding too little.

We have deliberately printed no rates or threshold figures here. Amazon revises them, and a number copied from a page that was right last year is how sellers end up with a wrong budget. The current figures, and which size tiers are covered, are on Amazon's own seller fee pages. Read them and check the date.

How Amazon decides you are low: days of cover

Amazon looks at your inventory relative to your recent demand. The idea is days of supply: how long your available units would last at your recent sales rate. It looks at that over a sustained period rather than a single day, so one bad week does not usually trigger it, but a pattern of running thin does.

You can calculate your own version to see it coming:

days of cover = units available (including units Amazon can sell now) ÷ average units sold per day

For example, if a SKU has 300 units available and sells 20 a day, it has 15 days of cover. If your supplier takes longer than that to deliver and get checked in, you will spend part of every cycle below whatever line Amazon applies. The fix is not a bigger order. It is an earlier one.

  • Use sales velocity that reflects now. A trailing average that includes a slow period understates how fast the SKU is moving after a promotion or a seasonal lift.
  • Count inbound carefully. Units that have not been received and checked in generally do not count as available stock, so a shipment in transit does not protect you yet.
  • Add your full lead time. Production, transit, receiving at the fulfilment centre. Cover has to survive all of it.

How to check what you have actually been charged

Do not rely on a feeling that you were charged. Check it.

  • Find the fee in Seller Central reports. Amazon provides a low-inventory-level fee report in the fulfilment reports area, with the fee broken down by SKU. Several third-party inventory tools also surface it. A simple search of Seller Central help for the report name will take you to it.
  • Match fee lines to SKUs. Most of the cost usually sits on a handful of fast sellers, not the whole catalogue.
  • Compare against your own stock history. For each charged SKU, look at the weeks before the charge. Was there a late shipment, a stockout, a promotion you did not plan stock for?
  • Check eligibility. The fee applies to some product types and size tiers and not others, and Amazon lists exemptions. If you were charged on something that looks exempt, that is a question for Seller Support.

This gives you a short list of SKUs and a cause for each, which is what you need to fix it.

A checklist to avoid the low inventory fee

  • Set a reorder point per SKU, not per catalogue. Reorder point = daily sales × (total lead time + a safety buffer). Fast sellers need a bigger buffer.
  • Reorder against current velocity. Re-run the sums when sales change, especially before and after promotions and seasonal peaks.
  • Send smaller shipments more often on fast sellers, if freight cost allows. It keeps cover steady without parking a large pile in a fulfilment centre.
  • Watch inbound, not just on-hand. A shipment stuck at receiving is not stock. Chase delays early.
  • Balance against storage fees. More stock lowers this fee but raises storage and aged inventory risk. The right level is the lowest cover that keeps you clear of the threshold, not the highest you can afford.
  • Review monthly. Put the fee report and a stock-cover list on one calendar reminder.
  • Decide in advance what to do when a SKU will miss: expedite, split the shipment, or accept the fee for a short period on purpose.

None of this needs a special tool. A spreadsheet handles a small catalogue. It becomes hard to keep up when the SKU count grows, lead times vary by supplier and several people touch purchasing.

Is the fee worth paying sometimes?

Occasionally, yes, and it is worth knowing when. The fee is a cost per unit shipped. A stockout is a cost too, in lost sales and in the ranking and momentum a listing can lose when it goes out of stock. If a late shipment is going to leave you thin for a short window, then paying the fee on purpose while you wait for stock can be cheaper than an emergency air freight bill or a stockout.

The key phrase is on purpose. A fee you chose to accept after doing the arithmetic is a business decision. A fee you discovered on a statement is a leak.

Run the comparison for any SKU that is going to miss: fee per unit multiplied by the units you expect to ship while low, versus the cost of faster freight, versus the margin on the sales you would lose in a stockout. Use your own margins. Do not assume the answer.

What to do with this

Before you compare subscription prices, price the leak. Open your FBA storage fee and aged-inventory surcharge lines for the last twelve months, add the units you were out of stock on your best sellers, and add the value of every SKU that has not moved in 180 days. That total is the number the purchase has to move. A cheaper seat that nobody has time to drive will not move it.

Common questions

What is the Amazon low inventory fee?

It is the low-inventory-level fee, an FBA charge that applies when your stock cover is thin compared with your sales for a sustained period. It is charged per unit shipped on eligible products. Amazon publishes the current rate and threshold on its seller fee pages.

How much is the Amazon low inventory level fee?

It depends on the product's size tier, how low your cover is and the year, and Amazon changes the rates. We print none here so that you do not budget from an out-of-date figure. Read the current rate card on Amazon's seller site, then multiply by the units you expect to ship while cover is low.

How do I avoid the low inventory fee on Amazon?

Keep days of cover above Amazon's threshold on your fast sellers. Calculate cover as available units divided by average daily sales, set a reorder point that includes your full lead time plus a buffer, and reorder against current velocity rather than an old average.

Where can I see the low inventory level fee report?

In Seller Central, in the fulfilment reports area, where Amazon lists the fee by SKU. If you cannot find it, search Seller Central help for the report name. Some inventory tools also show it alongside your other fees.

Can I get the low inventory fee refunded?

Not if it was charged correctly. If you were charged on a product that Amazon lists as exempt, or the stock data behind the charge was wrong, open a case with Seller Support and keep the case number. Filing windows are limited, so do it promptly.
