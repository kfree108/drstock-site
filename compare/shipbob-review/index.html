What ShipBob is, and what a review of it should actually answer

ShipBob is a 3PL. You ship your inventory into their fulfilment centres, they receive it, hold it, and then pick, pack and ship each order as it comes in. The dashboard and integrations come with it — ShipBob's own pricing page states the software platform is free for all customers, along with standard packaging: plain boxes, poly mailers, tape and labels.

That matters because most "ShipBob review" pages grade it as though it were software. It is not software. It is a warehouse network with software attached, and warehouses are judged on things software reviews never measure: how fast inventory gets received and made sellable, how accurate the picks are, where your units physically sit relative to your customers, and what happens when a pallet goes missing.

So the useful review question is not "is ShipBob good". It is: do you need someone else's warehouse network, and are you the kind of account that gets looked after inside one? Those are two different questions and the second one is where most of the disappointment lives.

Why the ratings split so hard, and what the hidden variable is

Go and read the reviews yourself on G2, Capterra and Trustpilot, and you will notice something odd. The distribution is not a bell curve. It is bimodal: a large block of five-star reviews and an unusually large block of one-star reviews, with very little in the middle. Two groups of people are describing what sounds like two different companies.

This is a property of the 3PL category rather than a fault unique to ShipBob, and the variable is almost always the same one: order volume, and whether you have a named human on the account. Above a certain monthly volume you get a merchant success manager, escalation paths and someone who answers when a shipment reconciles short. Below it you get a ticket queue. The product is identical. The experience is not remotely identical, and the reviews are honest reports from both sides of that line.

The practical move, before you sign anything, is to ask exactly where that line sits for your volume, get the answer in writing, and then filter the public reviews to accounts roughly your size. A five-star review from a brand doing 20,000 orders a month tells you very little about your first year at 400.

What ShipBob is genuinely very good at

We are not in the fulfilment business and have nothing to sell you here, so take this at face value. ShipBob is a strong choice when:

  • You sell on several channels and Amazon is only one of them. FBA is excellent inside Amazon and useless outside it. A 3PL gives you one pool of inventory serving your own site, wholesale and marketplaces.
  • You want to spread inventory across nodes to cut shipping zones. This is the real economic argument for a distributed 3PL and it is a genuine one — shipping cost is driven by weight, dimensions, service and distance, so moving units closer to demand moves the bill.
  • You do not want to run a warehouse. Leases, staff, WMS, insurance, peak-season hiring. Handing that to someone whose entire business is that job is frequently the right call.
  • You want fast onboarding and integrations that already exist. This is consistently what the positive reviews praise, along with the dashboard being more legible than most.
  • You need an FBM or DTC backstop. Holding a buffer outside Amazon so you can keep selling when FBA restock limits or an inbound delay bite is a sound reason to have a 3PL relationship, independent of everything else.

If several of those are true for you, the rest of this page will not change your mind and should not.

What a 3PL cannot fix, and where Amazon sellers get caught

Here is the honest boundary. Dr. Stock does not fulfil orders. We have no warehouses, we do not pick or pack, and if what you need is a fulfilment network then you need ShipBob or one of its peers and we are not an alternative to them in any sense. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling.

What a 3PL also does not do — because it is not their job — is the economics of your Amazon inventory. None of the following is touched by choosing a good 3PL:

  • FBA fee errors and dimensional-weight misclassification. Amazon records a size tier per ASIN. When the recorded dimensions do not match the measured product, you pay the wrong fulfilment fee on every unit, quietly, for as long as it goes unchecked. This is the single most checkable leak on this list.
  • Storage fees and the aged-inventory surcharge. Amazon's surcharge tiers begin at 181 days of storage and the charge is assessed against an inventory snapshot taken monthly. The decision — remove, liquidate, reprice, or accept the fee — has a deadline, and the deadline is not on anybody's dashboard.
  • Reorder timing and stockout risk. An out-of-stock hero SKU loses rank, and rank is expensive to buy back. The low-inventory-level fee then charges you for the same mistake from the other direction.
  • Shipment discrepancies and reimbursements. Inbound shipments that reconcile short, lost and damaged units, fee overcharges. Amazon auto-credits some of this now; inbound discrepancies and fee errors generally still need a human to open the case, and the claim windows are short — current policy describes most fulfilment-centre claims as filed within 60 days.
  • Cash trapped in slow-moving SKUs. The working-capital question. Every dollar sitting in a SKU that has not moved in 180 days is a dollar not buying the SKU that is selling out.

Nine checks to run before you sign with any 3PL

Take these to ShipBob and to every quote you put next to it. The point is to get two quotes onto the same basis, which is harder than it sounds because no two 3PLs describe their fees the same way.

  • Receiving turnaround. How many business days from truck arrival to sellable, guaranteed in writing, in peak as well as in March.
  • Pick accuracy and on-time ship rate. Ask for the actual figures, not the marketing ones, and ask how they are measured.
  • Order cut-off times per node, and what happens to an order placed twenty minutes late.
  • How storage is billed. Bin, shelf, pallet — and exactly how a unit is assigned to one. This is where quotes stop being comparable.
  • Dimensional weight rounding. Ask how they measure and round. On light, bulky products this decides the shipping line.
  • Node placement. Which warehouses will hold your inventory, and what your zone distribution looks like against your actual order map.
  • Minimums and account fees. Monthly minimums, implementation, and anything charged whether or not you ship.
  • Support tier at your volume. Named manager or ticket queue, and the exact threshold between them.
  • Offboarding. What it costs and how long it takes to get your inventory back out. Ask this before you sign; it is much less pleasant to ask later.

Which one you should actually pick

ShipBob suits multichannel DTC brands that want someone else running the warehouse and value fast onboarding and clean integrations — genuinely good at that job. Below its support threshold, expect ticket-queue service and price accordingly. It does not touch FBA fee errors, storage surcharges, stockouts or reimbursements, which is Dr. Stock's job. Many brands correctly buy both.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you need done, not the feature list. Pull your last three inbound shipment reconciliation reports and count the units received against units shipped, then pull your storage fees and aged-inventory surcharges for the last twelve months. Ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes those gaps, or only shows them to you on a dashboard.

Common questions

Is ShipBob worth it for Amazon sellers?

It depends what you want it to do. For fulfilling orders outside Amazon, holding a multichannel inventory pool, or keeping an FBM backstop when FBA restock limits bite, yes — that is a sound use of a 3PL. As a replacement for FBA on Amazon itself, be careful: you lose Prime badge economics and you take on the shipping cost yourself, so run the per-unit maths on your own products before switching anything material.

Why are ShipBob reviews so mixed?

Because the reviews come from accounts at wildly different volumes. The rating distribution across the major review sites is bimodal — a big five-star block and a big one-star block with little in between — and the variable is usually whether the account is large enough for a named merchant success manager rather than a ticket queue. Filter reviews to brands your size, and ask ShipBob directly where the support threshold sits for you.

Does ShipBob publish its prices?

No. Their pricing page names the fee categories — implementation, receiving, warehousing, and picking, packing and shipping each order, with returns, kitting and B2B or EDI orders charged additionally — but states that every quote is customised. That is normal for the category, since shipping cost genuinely does vary by weight, dimensions, destination and service. It does mean you cannot compare 3PLs without getting two quotes and normalising them yourself.

Can Dr. Stock replace ShipBob?

No, and we would not want you to think so. ShipBob physically stores and ships your products. Dr. Stock runs the economics of Amazon inventory — reorder timing, stockout risk, storage and aged-inventory fees, FBA fee errors and dimensional-weight misclassification, shipment discrepancies, reimbursement recovery, returns cost per SKU, and the cash trapped in slow-moving stock. Plenty of brands use both. They solve different problems and the answer to one is not the answer to the other.

What should I do first if my Amazon margin is slipping?

Before you change fulfilment partners, spend an hour finding out where the margin actually went. Pull twelve months of storage fees and aged-inventory surcharges, count the days your top five ASINs were out of stock, compare Amazon's recorded dimensions for those ASINs against your own measured dimensions, and reconcile your last three inbound shipments received-versus-shipped. If the money is in those four places, changing 3PL will not recover it. If the money is instead going out through the ad account, that is a different product — Dr. PPC.
