You are probably not choosing. You are considering leaving.

Almost nobody types this phrase from a standing start. They type it in month fourteen of a subscription, after a renewal email, or after a bad week where the tool did not answer the question they had. That is a different question from "which is better", and it deserves a different answer.

The honest one: if the tool you have is doing its job, switching will cost you more than it returns. These two products are far closer in capability than the comparison industry around them suggests, and the gap between them is smaller than the gap between a tool used weekly and the same tool used twice a quarter.

So before anything else, answer one question. In the last ninety days, how many decisions did your current subscription actually cause? Not sessions, not reports opened — decisions. If the number is healthy, stay. If it is zero, the problem is not which vendor you picked, and a new login will not fix it.

The switching cost is not the subscription

People price a switch at the difference between two monthly fees. That is the smallest part of it. The real bill looks like this:

  • Retraining. Two to four weeks before anyone is as fast in the new tool as they were in the old one. Multiply by however many people touch it.
  • Historical tracking resets. Rank history, tracked keyword series and product tracker data do not travel between vendors. You start the clock again, and seasonality comparisons you would have had in November are gone.
  • Saved work. Keyword lists, filters, saved searches and internal reporting built on one tool's exports have to be rebuilt against the other's.
  • Overlap. You will run both for at least a month. Budget for it rather than being surprised by it.
  • Annual commitments. If you took a twelve-month plan for the discount, the switch is not free until the term ends.

Add that up honestly. For a two-person team it is usually a few thousand dollars of time. That is the hurdle the switch has to clear — not $30 a month.

The three differences that survive a real workweek

Strip out the feature-count noise and three things actually change your experience.

1. Marketplace breadth. Helium 10 covers more marketplaces and more non-Amazon channels, including Walmart and TikTok Shop. Jungle Scout is more tightly focused on Amazon. If you only sell on Amazon US this is irrelevant. If you are opening a second channel next quarter it is the whole decision. Verify the current lists on both vendors' pages — coverage changes several times a year.

2. Ads bundling and how it is billed. Helium 10 Diamond includes Helium 10 Ads, and their pricing page states a 2% management fee on PPC spend, described in their documentation as applying to spend in profiles set to Managed above a monthly threshold. Jungle Scout does not bundle ad management. Neither approach is wrong, and a percentage of ad spend is a normal, defensible way to price the work — we charge one on Dr. PPC. But it means Helium 10's true cost scales with your advertising and Jungle Scout's does not, so the cheaper option flips depending on your spend.

3. Seat maths. Jungle Scout charges $49 a month per additional seat; Helium 10 bundles users into the tier — one additional user on Platinum, up to five on Diamond, with connected-account limits alongside. A solo seller and a five-person team should reach opposite conclusions from the same two price lists. Figures read from both vendors' own pages on 20 August 2026, in US dollars.

The switch that usually pays, and the one that usually does not

Usually pays: moving up into Helium 10 when your team has grown, you have added a marketplace, and someone is in the tool most days. The depth is real, the extra keyword surface area gets used, and the cost of the switch is amortised across several people who will each get faster.

Usually pays: moving down to Jungle Scout when you realise you are paying for a suite to use three tools in it. Simplification is a legitimate reason to switch and nobody should feel sheepish about it. If your genuine use is opportunity screening and a restock forecast, you are overpaying and the cheaper tool will get used more.

Usually does not pay: switching because the current tool did not answer a question that neither tool can answer. This is the most common switch we see and it is always a disappointment, because the reader has an operating problem and is shopping for a research product. No vendor in this category can fix a margin that is leaking through fulfilment fees, storage surcharges, stockouts or unclaimed shipments, and swapping one for the other buys you a fresh interface and the same quarter.

If neither switch fixes your quarter

Run this check before you move a subscription. Four numbers, an hour of work, and it will tell you whether you are shopping in the right aisle at all.

  • Twelve months of storage fees plus aged-inventory surcharges. Amazon's surcharge tiers begin at 181 days of storage.
  • The true cost of returns per SKU — not the refund, but the refund plus the return fee plus the unit that came back unsellable. This is the number most brands have never actually calculated, and on some categories it quietly inverts the profitability ranking of the catalogue.
  • Cash sitting in SKUs with no movement in 180 days. That is working capital you already paid for and cannot spend.
  • Days out of stock on your top five ASINs, and any low-inventory-level fees that came with them.

If that total is larger than your annual software spend — and for most brands past their first million it is, by some distance — the subscription question is not the important one on your desk. Which one you should actually pick

Stay where you are unless something specific has changed: a new marketplace, a bigger team, or bundled ads you will actually use. Helium 10 rewards daily users and multichannel sellers; Jungle Scout rewards teams who want fewer moving parts. If the real problem is fees, returns and trapped cash, no switch between them reaches it — Dr. Stock does.

What to do with this

Neither of these files a fee dispute for you. Before you pick, run one check: take your ten highest-volume ASINs, compare Amazon's recorded package dimensions against your own measured dimensions, and flag anything where the size tier looks wrong. Then ask each vendor what happens next — who measures, who files, who follows up.

Common questions

Should I switch from Helium 10 to Jungle Scout?

Only if you are paying for a suite and using three tools in it, or if the interface is stopping your team from using it at all. Simplification is a good reason. Price alone usually is not, because the switching cost — retraining, lost rank history, rebuilt keyword lists, a month of overlap — is typically worth more than the monthly difference. Count the decisions your current tool caused last quarter first.

Do I lose my data if I switch?

You keep anything you exported, and you lose the accumulated tracking. Rank history, tracked keyword series and product tracker data do not transfer between vendors, so seasonality comparisons restart from your switch date. If you are going to move, export everything you care about first and try to switch at the start of a quarter rather than in the middle of Q4.

Is Helium 10 worth the extra money over Jungle Scout?

It is if you will use the depth: more keyword surface area, more granular rank tracking, broader marketplace coverage, and bundled ad management on Diamond. It is not if one person opens it twice a month. The cost that decides it is usually not the subscription but the ad management fee — Helium 10 Diamond's 2% on managed PPC spend scales with your advertising, so run it at your actual monthly spend before comparing.

Can I run both without paying twice?

Not indefinitely, but a one-month overlap is the right way to make the decision rather than a waste. Both sell month to month and Jungle Scout offers a seven-day money-back guarantee on Catalyst, so a short paid test on your own ASINs will tell you more than any comparison page. Just do not let the overlap drift into a permanent second subscription nobody opens.

What if my problem is not research at all?

Then no switch here helps, and this is more common than the search volume suggests.
