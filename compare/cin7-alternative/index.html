Four directions, and only one of them is a vendor comparison

Inventory systems are unusually hard to shop for, because the alternatives are not all the same kind of thing. A larger platform, a peer platform, a lighter stack and no platform at all are four different decisions with four different price shapes, and most comparison pages mix them into a single ranked list as though they were interchangeable.

For the baseline, since a total-cost comparison needs one: Cin7 Core's published tiers on the vendor's own pricing page, read on 20 August 2026 in US dollars, are $349, $599 and $1,199 a month, banded by annual sales order volume at 6,000, 24,000 and 120,000 respectively, with 5, 10 and 15 users. Cin7 Omni, the larger retail and distribution platform, is quoted on a call.

Two things follow from that structure. First, the band is annual sales orders, so a strong year can move you up a tier without anything about your needs changing — which is one of the most common reasons people start reading pages like this one. Second, the licence is only part of the number; implementation and the internal hours to run it are the rest, and they do not appear on anybody's pricing page.

So before any shortlist, answer one question: which direction am I moving, and why? The four sections below are the four answers.

Up: when a full ERP is genuinely the right answer

You move up when the constraint has stopped being inventory and started being the business. The honest signals are specific:

  • Multiple legal entities or currencies that need consolidated financial reporting.
  • Manufacturing complexity beyond assembly — multi-level bills of materials, work orders, production scheduling, quality control.
  • Finance requirements your accounting platform can no longer carry: revenue recognition, project accounting, statutory reporting across jurisdictions.
  • An investor, acquirer or auditor asking questions your current stack cannot answer without a spreadsheet.

NetSuite is the reference point in this tier, alongside the mid-market offerings from the large enterprise software vendors. None of them publishes a usable price — the model is licence plus modules plus users plus an implementation partner, quoted per customer — and implementation is a project measured in quarters with a named internal owner and, usually, a paid partner firm.

Move up only if two or more of those signals are genuinely true. The failure mode here is buying an enterprise platform to fix an operational problem, which converts a frustrating quarter into an expensive year. If your reason for looking is a support ticket that went unanswered, this is not your direction.

Sideways: peer platforms, and the warning that comes with them

This is the direction most people assume they are taking. The peer set is real and worth knowing:

  • Extensiv — strongest where multichannel order management and third-party warehouse operations are the centre of gravity, particularly for brands working with or running fulfilment networks. The order management product is the descendant of what was sold as Skubana.
  • Katana — built around make-to-order and light manufacturing, with a production floor view that generalists lack.
  • Unleashed and Fishbowl — established mid-market inventory platforms with strong purchasing, assembly and accounting integration, each with its own regional and vertical strengths.
  • Zoho Inventory — considerably cheaper, genuinely capable at the smaller end, and the natural landing spot if you are already inside that vendor's wider suite.
  • Linnworks — marketplace-listing-heavy operations where channel management is the dominant problem rather than manufacturing.

Now the warning, because it is the most useful sentence on this page. If your current implementation stalled, a sideways move will usually stall too. Across the failures we have watched, the cause is rarely the software. It is unclean product data, cost history that was never reconciled, a workflow nobody has written down, and no named internal owner with time protected for the project. Every one of those follows you to the next vendor, and you will have paid a migration to rediscover them.

Before moving sideways, run a fair test: give the current system one owned, time-boxed eight weeks with clean data and a written workflow. If it still fails, move with confidence and a much better requirements list. If it succeeds, you have saved yourself two quarters.

Down: the lighter stack, and who it actually suits

Moving down is unfashionable and frequently correct. A meaningful number of brands running a full inventory platform are using perhaps a fifth of it, because they bought for a complexity that either never arrived or has since simplified.

The lighter stack is usually three things: your accounting platform doing what it is good at, a channel or listing tool where you actually sell, and a disciplined purchasing process that lives in one shared document with one owner. It sounds primitive next to a platform demo. For a single-channel brand with a modest SKU count, no manufacturing and no wholesale, it is often more accurate, because the number of places data can drift out of sync drops to almost none.

The honest signals that down is your direction:

  • One dominant sales channel, with anything else immaterial.
  • No manufacturing, no kitting beyond simple bundles, no serial or batch traceability requirement.
  • A SKU count a person can hold in their head, and suppliers you order from on a predictable rhythm.
  • Nobody has opened the more advanced modules in six months.

The trade you are making is ceiling for simplicity, and you should make it consciously — with a written note of the trigger that would send you back up, so the decision gets revisited on evidence rather than on the day something breaks.

Out: when the problem was never in the inventory system

The fourth direction is the one nobody sells you, so we will be explicit about our own position first. Dr. Stock is not a system of record and not an ERP. We do not replace an inventory platform, hold your purchase order workflow or reconcile your accounts. If you need one, buy one from the directions above.

But there is a specific and common case where the shopping trip is misdirected: a brand where the overwhelming majority of volume is Amazon FBA, margin is thinning, and the inventory system is being blamed because it is the most visible line item in operations. In that case the platform is usually working fine, and the money is going somewhere it was never designed to look:

  • Fulfilment charged from a size tier that no longer matches the packed unit — accurate stock records, wrong fee, on every unit, indefinitely.
  • Storage that escalates with age, with a surcharge attaching past 181 days and steepening in bands beyond that, assessed on a monthly snapshot. Slow stock therefore carries a decision date that no system of record will raise with you.
  • Rank lost to a stockout and then rebought through advertising, a cost that almost never gets booked against the stockout that caused it.
  • Claims never filed — inbound shipments received short, units lost or damaged, fee overcharges, refunds where nothing came back — against windows measured in weeks.
  • Returns costed at the refund rather than at the fulfilment fee already spent plus processing plus write-off plus replacement.

If you are migrating: protect stock accuracy above everything

Whichever direction you choose, a migration goes wrong in one specific way — your stock position stops being true, and you find out from a customer. Sequence to prevent that:

  • Clean the data before you choose the vendor. Deduplicate SKUs, reconcile cost history, fix units of measure and retire dead products. Do it now and it improves the current system too; do it later and it becomes the migration's critical path.
  • Write the workflow down first. Purchase to receipt to sale to reconciliation, on one page, as it actually happens rather than as it is meant to. This is the requirements document, and it is what stops you buying features nobody will use.
  • Name the owner and protect their time. Not a committee. One person with hours removed from their normal week.
  • Count physical stock at cutover. A full count is the only way to start the new system with a true opening position, and starting with a false one poisons every report for a year.
  • Run parallel through one full cycle — one purchase, one receipt, one settlement, one reconciliation — before switching off the old system.
  • Never cut over into peak. The quiet part of your year exists for this.
  • Fix the Amazon-side leaks in parallel. They are unaffected by the migration, they recover money during a period when nothing else improves, and if you skip them they will still be there under the new logo.

Which one you should actually pick

Move up to an ERP only for multi-entity finance or real manufacturing. Move sideways for a genuine capability gap, never to escape a stalled implementation — the causes travel with you. Move down if one channel dominates and nobody opens the advanced modules. And if most of your volume is FBA and margin is thinning, the alternative you need is not another platform at all.

What to do with this

Before you switch, write down the one number the switch has to change — reorder timing, storage fees, fee misclassification, or reimbursements recovered. Then ask the new vendor to show you how their product moves that specific number, and what happens on the weeks nobody logs in.

Common questions

What are the main alternatives to Cin7?

They fall into tiers rather than a single list. Above it sit full ERPs such as NetSuite, quoted per customer and implemented over quarters. Alongside it sit peer platforms including Extensiv, Katana, Unleashed, Fishbowl, Zoho Inventory and Linnworks, each with a different centre of gravity — warehouse operations, light manufacturing, purchasing, or marketplace listing. Below it sits a deliberately lighter stack. Which tier is relevant depends on why you are moving, not on which name ranks highest.

Is switching inventory systems worth it?

It is when there is a genuine capability gap — you need manufacturing depth, EDI, multi-entity accounting or warehouse features your current platform does not have. It usually is not when the trigger is a stalled implementation, because the causes of those are unclean data, undocumented workflow and no named owner, and all three migrate with you. Give the current system one owned, time-boxed eight weeks with clean data before you commit to a move.

Do I need an inventory management system if I only sell on Amazon?

Often not. If close to all your volume is FBA, you have no manufacturing and no wholesale, an inventory platform is solving distribution problems you do not have while your actual costs sit inside Amazon's fee structure. Your accounting platform, your channel tooling and a disciplined purchasing process may cover it. Revisit the decision if you add wholesale, retail, your own site at scale, or assembly.

What does an inventory system migration actually cost?

Far more than the licence difference. Budget the implementation fee, the internal hours of whoever owns the project, a full physical stock count at cutover, a parallel-running period across at least one complete purchase-to-reconciliation cycle, and retraining everyone who touches stock. The largest hidden cost is data cleaning — deduplicating SKUs and reconciling cost history — which is worth doing regardless, because it improves whichever system you end up on.
