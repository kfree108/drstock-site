Two products wear the same name

The first thing to fix before reading any Cin7 review, including this one, is which product it is about. Reviews on the software directories are frequently filed against one and quoted about the other, which is how you end up with a page that praises the ease of setup and condemns the complexity of setup in the same breath.

Cin7 Core is the product formerly sold as DEAR Systems, aimed at growing small and mid-sized businesses. It has published pricing: on Cin7's own pricing page, read on 20 August 2026 in US dollars, Standard is $349 a month with 5 users, Pro is $599 a month with 10 users and Advanced is $1,199 a month with 15 users.

Cin7 Omni is the larger platform, listed with 8 users and flexible order volume, and priced on a call rather than on the page. It is where the heavier retail and distribution capability lives — EDI trading with big-box retailers, point of sale, more complex multi-warehouse and multi-entity work. Publishing no price at that level is standard for the category and is a finding rather than a criticism: it means you cannot budget without a conversation.

Getting this right matters because the two products suit different businesses. A brand that is essentially ecommerce plus a little wholesale is a Core buyer. A brand shipping to national retail chains on EDI is not, and would be badly served by a review of the smaller product.

What you are actually reviewing is a commitment

Most software you buy can be abandoned in an afternoon. An inventory management system cannot, and that is the fact that should shape the whole evaluation.

Once it is live, this system becomes your source of truth for stock. Your purchase orders originate in it. Your accounting reconciles to it. Your channels sync from it. Your warehouse staff work in it. Two years later it holds your cost history, your supplier terms, your bills of materials and every batch you have ever received. Replacing it at that point is not a software purchase, it is a project with a steering group.

So the review question is not "is the interface nice". It is: would we be content running our operation on this in four years, at three times our current volume, with people we have not hired yet? That reframes everything. Data model quality matters more than screen design. The quality of the partner network matters more than any individual feature. Whether your accountant already knows the system matters enormously, because they will be living in the reconciliation.

It also reframes the cost. The subscription is the smaller number. The larger one is the implementation: mapping SKUs, cleaning cost data, rebuilding purchase workflows, connecting channels, and retraining everyone who touches stock. Reviewers routinely describe multi-month onboarding for businesses with genuinely multichannel operations, and that is not a failure of the vendor — it is the honest duration of the work. Any inventory system will demand it. Budget the time as seriously as you budget the licence.

What Cin7 is genuinely strong at

We do not sell an inventory management system and have no stake in this, so take it at face value: for the businesses it is built for, Cin7 is a capable and well-established platform, and the category leader in several of these areas is not obviously better.

  • One stock pool across many channels. Ecommerce, marketplaces, wholesale, retail and physical locations reconciled against a single inventory position. If you are currently maintaining stock in three systems and a spreadsheet, this is the problem it solves and it solves it properly.
  • Purchasing and supply workflow. Purchase orders, supplier management, landed cost allocation, batch and serial tracking, and light manufacturing with bills of materials. This is considerably deeper than what any Amazon-focused tool offers.
  • Accounting integration. Tight connections to the mainstream accounting platforms, which is what makes it a system of record rather than a dashboard, and is why finance teams often drive the selection.
  • B2B and wholesale. Price tiers by customer, sales rep workflows, and on Omni the EDI trading that selling into large retailers requires.
  • Warehouse and 3PL connectivity. Bin locations, pick and pack workflow, and integrations to third-party logistics providers so an outsourced warehouse still reports into your source of truth.

If you are running genuine multichannel distribution and the wheels are coming off your spreadsheets, this is the right category of purchase and Cin7 belongs on your shortlist.

Reading the reviews properly

Cin7's public ratings sit respectably in the middle-to-upper range across the major directories, with hundreds of reviews behind them. The complaints cluster, and the clusters are more informative than the score:

  • Support response times on complex issues. The most consistent criticism, and worth probing directly during the sales process. Ask what the response commitment is at your tier, and what the escalation path looks like for something that breaks stock accuracy on a Friday.
  • Billing and commercial complexity. Order volume bands, user counts, module add-ons and implementation packages combine into a bill that is harder to predict than the pricing page implies. Ask for a twelve-month total in writing, including implementation and every module you have been shown.
  • Onboarding length. Reported in months rather than weeks for multichannel businesses. Read this as the true shape of the work rather than as a vendor failing — but do read it, and staff accordingly.
  • Reviews that describe two different products. Filter by which platform the reviewer is on before you weight their opinion.

What is largely absent from the criticism is anyone saying the core inventory logic is unreliable, and for a system of record that is the review that matters most. The pattern says: capable product, real implementation effort, plan your support expectations.

One practical filter: weight reviews from businesses whose channel mix looks like yours. A wholesale-heavy distributor and an Amazon-first brand will report entirely different experiences of the same software, and both will be telling the truth.

The boundary: a system of record does not defend your Amazon margin

Now the honest limit, and we will state our own side of it first. Dr. Stock is not a system of record and it is not an ERP. We do not want to own your purchase order workflow, your bills of materials, your wholesale price tiers or your accounting reconciliation. If that is what you need, buy an inventory system; we are not an alternative to one and would not pretend to be.

What an inventory system does not do — because it is not what it is for — is defend the margin on the Amazon side of your business:

  • Fulfilment fees priced from the wrong size tier. Amazon charges from the dimensions it has recorded, not from the ones in your product master. When those disagree, your inventory system will show a perfectly accurate stock position while you overpay on every unit that ships.
  • The ageing clock inside Amazon's network. Storage compounds, and a surcharge attaches to units held past 181 days with further bands as stock gets older, assessed against a monthly snapshot. An IMS records where units are. It does not decide, before the snapshot, whether they should be removed, liquidated or discounted.
  • Reorder timing against Amazon's specific penalties. Out of stock costs you rank, which is then bought back through advertising. Too lean and a low-inventory-level charge lands instead. Hitting that band requires acting on Amazon's own signals, not just knowing your quantity on hand.
  • Money Amazon owes you. Inbound shipments received short, units lost or damaged, fee overcharges, refunds where the item never came back. Your system of record will happily show the discrepancy and will not file the claim, and the windows are short.
  • Returns at true cost. The refund is the small part. The fulfilment fee already spent, the return processing, the write-off and the replacement are the rest, and they belong in the SKU-level decision about what to keep selling.

Seven things to settle before you sign

These apply to any inventory system, not just this one, and they are the questions that decide whether year two is pleasant.

  • Which product am I being quoted? Core or Omni, and why that one. Get the reasoning in writing, because it determines your ceiling.
  • What is the twelve-month total? Licence, implementation, modules, additional users and any order-volume overage. One number, in writing.
  • Who owns the implementation? The vendor, a partner, or your team. Name the person. Unowned implementations are the single most common way these projects stall.
  • What is my order volume trajectory against the band? The band is priced annually, so a good year moves you up a tier. Model it before you sign rather than discovering it at renewal.
  • How does it handle my Amazon data specifically? Settlement reconciliation, fee-level detail, FBA inbound and removals. Ask for a demonstration on your own data, not a generic one.
  • What is the support commitment at my tier — response times, hours, and the escalation path when stock accuracy is affected.
  • What does leaving look like? Data export formats, notice period, and whether your cost history comes out in a usable shape. Ask this before you sign; it is a much less comfortable question later.

If the answers are solid, this is a sound category of purchase. If several of them come back vague, that is information about how year two will feel.

Which one you should actually pick

Cin7 Core is a capable system of record for multichannel businesses outgrowing spreadsheets, with real implementation effort attached and support expectations worth setting during the sales process. Omni is the heavier retail and EDI product, quoted on a call. Neither is built to defend Amazon margin — fee errors, ageing stock and unclaimed money sit outside the category entirely.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you need done, not the feature list. Pull your last three inbound shipment reconciliation reports and count the units received against units shipped, then pull your storage fees and aged-inventory surcharges for the last twelve months. Ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes those gaps, or only shows them to you on a dashboard.

Common questions

What is the difference between Cin7 Core and Cin7 Omni?

Core is the product previously sold as DEAR Systems, aimed at growing small and mid-sized businesses, and it carries published pricing by tier. Omni is the larger platform for more complex retail and distribution operations — EDI trading, point of sale, heavier multi-warehouse work — and it is quoted on a call rather than priced on the page. They are different products for different businesses, and a review of one tells you little about the other.

Is Cin7 good for Amazon sellers?

It is good for Amazon sellers who are also something else — wholesale, retail, their own site, or manufacturing. In that situation you genuinely need one stock pool and one source of truth, and this category of product is the answer. If close to all of your volume is FBA and you have no manufacturing or wholesale complexity, you may be buying a distribution system to solve a marketplace problem, and the cost and implementation time will be hard to justify.

How long does a Cin7 implementation take?

Reviewers with multichannel operations commonly describe onboarding in months rather than weeks, and that is a fair reflection of what the work involves rather than a mark against the vendor: SKU mapping, cost history, channel connections, purchasing workflow and staff training. The variable that most affects the duration is not the software but whether a named person owns the project internally. Unowned implementations stall regardless of which system you picked.

Is Cin7 an ERP?

It sits in the space between inventory management and full ERP — deeper than a marketplace tool, lighter than a large enterprise resource planning suite, and generally paired with a separate accounting platform rather than replacing it. For most growing product businesses that is the right shape. If you need multi-entity consolidation, complex financials and full enterprise resource planning, you are shopping in the tier above this one.
